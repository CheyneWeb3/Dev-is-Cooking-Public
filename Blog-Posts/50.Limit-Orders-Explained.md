## Welcome to Devis Cooking Trading 101

Welcome back to the **Devis Cooking Crypto & Trading Education Series**, where we break down crypto, blockchain and trading concepts into straightforward lessons without unnecessary jargon.

In our previous lesson, we looked at **market orders**.

A market order essentially tells an exchange:

> **“Make this trade now using the best price currently available.”**

A **limit order** works differently.

Instead of letting the market decide what price you receive, you tell the market the price you are prepared to accept.

So let's cook it down.

---

# What Is a Limit Order?

A **limit order** is an instruction to buy or sell an asset at a specific price — or better.

Instead of saying:

> “Buy this now.”

you are saying:

> **“Buy this only if I can get my chosen price or better.”**

And when selling:

> **“Sell this only if I can receive my chosen price or better.”**

This gives you much more control over your execution price.

But there is a trade-off.

## Your order might never execute.

That is the fundamental difference between a market order and a limit order.

---

# A Simple Example

Imagine Bitcoin is currently trading at:

## **$100,000**

You believe $100,000 is a little expensive and would rather buy if Bitcoin drops to:

## **$95,000**

Instead of watching the chart all day, you could place a:

## **Buy Limit Order at $95,000**

Your exchange can then leave that order waiting.

If Bitcoin falls to $95,000 and enough Bitcoin becomes available at that price, your order may execute.

If Bitcoin instead rises to:

**$105,000**

then:

**$110,000**

then:

**$120,000**

your $95,000 limit order may simply remain unfilled.

You controlled your price.

But you did not guarantee that you would get the asset.

---

# Buy Limit Orders

A **buy limit order** sets the maximum price you are willing to pay.

Suppose a token currently trades at:

## **$10**

You would like to buy 100 tokens, but only if the price reaches:

## **$8**

You place:

**Buy 100 tokens**

**Limit price: $8**

This means:

> **“Buy these tokens at $8 or cheaper — but do not pay more than $8.”**

If the market falls to $8, your order may begin executing.

If there are sellers available at $7.95, you could even receive a better price.

That is why a limit order is generally described as executing at:

## **Your limit price or better**

---

# Sell Limit Orders

A **sell limit order** works in the opposite direction.

Suppose you own a token currently trading at:

## **$10**

You would like to sell if it reaches:

## **$15**

You could place:

**Sell 100 tokens**

**Limit price: $15**

You are telling the exchange:

> **“Sell my tokens for $15 or more. Do not sell below $15.”**

If the market reaches $15 and there are buyers available, your order may execute.

If the token only reaches $14.90 before falling again, your order may remain open.

---

# Limit Orders Give You Price Control

This is the biggest advantage.

With a market order, you prioritise:

## **Execution**

With a limit order, you prioritise:

## **Price**

Think of it this way:

### Market Order

**“I want to trade now.”**

### Limit Order

**“I want to trade — but only at my price.”**

That difference affects almost everything about how the order behaves.

---

# Market Order vs Limit Order

Let's compare them directly.

| Feature | Market Order | Limit Order |
|---|---|---|
| Main priority | Fast execution | Price control |
| Price guaranteed? | No | Limit price or better |
| Execution guaranteed? | Usually more likely | No |
| Can remain open? | Usually no | Yes |
| Useful for | Immediate trades | Planned entries and exits |

Neither one is automatically better.

They solve different problems.

---

# What Happens After You Place a Limit Order?

On a traditional order-book exchange, your limit order may be added to the:

## **Order Book**

The order book contains buying and selling interest at different prices.

For example:

### Sell Orders

| Price | Amount |
|---:|---:|
| $10.20 | 100 tokens |
| $10.10 | 200 tokens |
| $10.05 | 500 tokens |

### Buy Orders

| Price | Amount |
|---:|---:|
| $9.95 | 300 tokens |
| $9.90 | 400 tokens |
| $9.80 | 700 tokens |

The highest buyer may currently be offering:

**$9.95**

while the cheapest seller wants:

**$10.05**

That gap is known as the:

## **Bid-Ask Spread**

If you submit a buy limit at $9.90, your order may sit alongside other buyers waiting for a seller to accept that price.

---

# What Is a Maker?

You may hear exchanges talk about:

**Maker fees**

and

**Taker fees**

Limit orders help explain these terms.

When you place an order that sits on the order book and provides liquidity, you are generally acting as a:

## **Maker**

You are helping **make the market** by adding an available order.

Someone who immediately trades against an existing order is generally called a:

## **Taker**

They are **taking liquidity** that was already available.

Different exchanges may charge different fees to makers and takers.

---

# But Not Every Limit Order Becomes a Maker Order

This is an important detail.

Imagine Bitcoin is currently available for sale at:

## **$100,000**

You place a buy limit order at:

## **$101,000**

You may think:

> “It's a limit order, so it will sit there waiting.”

But because sellers already exist below your maximum price, the exchange may execute the order immediately.

Your limit means:

> **“Do not pay more than $101,000.”**

It does not mean:

> **“Wait until the market reaches exactly $101,000.”**

If Bitcoin can already be bought for $100,000, then that satisfies your limit.

---

# A Limit Price Is a Boundary, Not Necessarily an Exact Execution Price

This catches many beginners out.

Suppose you place:

## Buy Limit: $10

That means:

**Buy at $10 or lower.**

You might execute at:

- $10.00
- $9.99
- $9.95
- or another better available price

Likewise:

## Sell Limit: $15

means:

**Sell at $15 or higher.**

You may receive:

- $15.00
- $15.05
- $15.20

depending on the available market.

---

# Why Might a Limit Order Not Fill?

Several things can prevent execution.

## 1. The Market Never Reaches Your Price

You set a Bitcoin buy order at $80,000.

Bitcoin never falls below $95,000.

Your order stays open.

Simple.

---

## 2. The Market Touches Your Price but There Isn't Enough Liquidity

Imagine you want to buy:

**10,000 tokens at $1.00**

but only:

**500 tokens**

are available at that price.

You may receive a:

## **Partial Fill**

Some of your order executes while the rest remains waiting.

---

## 3. Other Orders Are Ahead of You

You are not necessarily first in line.

If other traders already placed orders at the same price, the exchange may process those first according to its matching rules.

So seeing the market reach your limit price does not always guarantee your entire order will fill.

---

# What Is a Partial Fill?

A **partial fill** happens when only part of your order can execute.

Suppose you place:

## Buy 1,000 tokens at $2

But only:

## 300 tokens

are available at $2.

The exchange may purchase those 300 tokens.

Your remaining:

## 700 tokens

could stay open waiting for additional sellers.

Your order is therefore:

**Partially filled**

rather than completely filled.

---

# Example: Buying a Pullback

Imagine ETH is currently trading at:

## **$4,000**

You believe that:

## **$3,800**

would be a better entry.

Instead of constantly watching the chart, you could place:

**Buy Limit**

**ETH**

**Price: $3,800**

If ETH falls to your chosen level, the exchange can attempt to execute your order.

This is one reason limit orders are useful for traders who plan entries ahead of time.

---

# Example: Taking Profit

Limit orders can also be used to plan exits.

Imagine you bought a token at:

## **$5**

You decide that if it reaches:

## **$7**

you want to sell 50% of your position.

You could place a:

## **Sell Limit at $7**

If the market reaches $7 and your order receives a fill, part of your position is sold automatically.

You don't necessarily need to be sitting in front of the chart when it happens.

---

# Can You Place Multiple Limit Orders?

Yes.

Some traders divide a planned entry or exit across several prices.

For example:

**Buy 25% at $10**

**Buy 25% at $9**

**Buy 25% at $8**

**Buy 25% at $7**

This is sometimes called:

## **Scaling into a position**

The same idea can be used for exits.

For example:

**Sell 25% at $15**

**Sell 25% at $20**

**Sell 25% at $25**

**Sell 25% at $30**

This gives the trader a predefined structure rather than relying entirely on emotional decisions in the moment.

It does not guarantee a better outcome, but it can help make a trading plan more deliberate.

---

# Limit Orders and Volatile Crypto Markets

Crypto markets can move extremely quickly.

A token may move:

**$10 → $12 → $8 → $11**

in a short period.

Limit orders can help traders establish prices they are prepared to accept before that volatility happens.

But there is an important downside.

You can also be left behind.

Imagine a token trades at:

## $10

You place a buy limit at:

## $9

The price then jumps:

**$10 → $12 → $15 → $20**

Your $9 order never executes.

You may have avoided paying $10.

But you also never entered the trade.

This is the price-control versus execution trade-off again.

---

# Limit Orders on Decentralised Exchanges

The situation becomes more complicated when we move into decentralised finance.

Many classic automated market maker DEXs don't operate with a traditional central limit order book.

Instead, traders swap against:

## **Liquidity Pools**

However, decentralised trading platforms can still offer limit-order-like functionality through:

- specialised smart contracts
- limit-order protocols
- concentrated liquidity systems
- automated execution services
- decentralised order books

The interface may feel similar:

> **“Execute my swap if TOKEN A reaches this price.”**

But under the hood, the mechanism may be very different from a traditional centralised exchange order book.

---

# Why This Matters

When using any platform, it is worth understanding what its **Limit Order** button actually does.

Questions worth checking include:

- Is my order stored on-chain?
- Is it stored off-chain until execution?
- Does a smart contract hold my assets?
- Can I cancel it?
- Are there execution fees?
- Is gas required?
- Does the order expire?
- What liquidity source executes it?
- Can it partially fill?

The button may say **Limit Order**, but the implementation can vary significantly between platforms.

---

# Limit Orders Don't Remove Trading Risk

A limit order protects your chosen price boundary.

It does not protect you from making a bad trade.

Imagine a token falls from:

**$10 to $8**

Your buy limit at $8 executes.

Then the token continues falling:

**$8 → $5 → $2**

Your limit order worked perfectly.

The trade itself still lost money.

This distinction matters.

## An order type controls execution.

## It does not predict the market.

---

# What Is a Good Limit Price?

There is no universal answer.

Traders may base limit orders on things such as:

- support levels
- resistance levels
- previous highs and lows
- liquidity areas
- trend structure
- their own valuation
- risk/reward calculations
- predetermined entry or exit plans

A limit order is simply the mechanism used to express that decision.

Choosing the price is still the trader's responsibility.

---

# Common Beginner Mistake: Setting a Buy Limit Above the Market

Suppose a token currently trades at:

## $10

You enter:

## Buy Limit: $12

You might think this means:

> “Buy when the token rises to $12.”

It doesn't.

A standard buy limit means:

> **“Buy for $12 or less.”**

Since the token is already available around $10, the order may execute immediately.

If you specifically want an order to activate when price rises above a certain point, you are moving into:

## **Stop Orders**

We'll cover those separately.

---

# Common Beginner Mistake: Setting a Sell Limit Below the Market

Likewise, imagine the token currently trades at:

## $10

and you enter:

## Sell Limit: $8

That means:

> **“Sell for $8 or better.”**

Since buyers may already exist around $10, the order could execute immediately.

A limit order is not simply:

> “Do something when the price reaches this number.”

The direction and order type matter.

---

# Time in Force

Many exchanges also ask:

## How long should this limit order remain active?

This is called:

## **Time in Force**

Common examples include:

### Good 'Til Cancelled — GTC

The order remains active until it fills or you cancel it.

### Immediate or Cancel — IOC

Execute whatever portion can be filled immediately and cancel the remainder.

### Fill or Kill — FOK

The entire order must execute immediately or the whole order is cancelled.

Different exchanges may provide different options.

For beginners, understanding that limit orders can remain active for long periods is particularly important.

---

# Don't Forget About Old Orders

This sounds obvious, but it causes real mistakes.

Imagine you placed a buy limit months ago and forgot about it.

The market later crashes.

Your forgotten order suddenly executes.

Now you own an asset you weren't expecting to buy.

So regularly review:

- open orders
- partially filled orders
- old take-profit orders
- old buy orders
- available account balance

An order doesn't forget just because you do.

---

# Limit Orders and Liquidity

Limit orders themselves can become part of market liquidity.

Suppose thousands of traders have buy limits around:

## **$50**

That can create significant buying interest around that price.

Likewise, large sell orders clustered around:

## **$60**

can create substantial selling pressure.

This is one reason traders study:

## **Order Book Depth**

It helps show where buying and selling interest currently exists.

But remember:

Orders can be cancelled.

An order book shows current intentions — not guaranteed future behaviour.

---

# Limit Orders Can Reduce Slippage — But Not Always

A major advantage of a limit order is that you define the worst acceptable execution price.

If you set:

## Buy Limit: $100

the exchange should not execute that standard limit order above $100.

This can prevent the kind of uncontrolled price movement that can occur with a large market order.

However, you may receive:

- only a partial fill
- no fill at all
- execution over a longer period

Again, you're trading execution certainty for price control.

---

# Market Order or Limit Order?

A simple way to think about the choice is:

## Use a Market Order When:

The priority is:

**“I want to trade now.”**

## Use a Limit Order When:

The priority is:

**“I only want this trade at this price or better.”**

Neither choice should be automatic.

Consider:

**Liquidity**

**Volatility**

**Trade size**

**Fees**

**Spread**

**Urgency**

and your overall trading plan.

---

# Before Placing a Limit Order

Check:

- the current market price
- whether you're buying or selling
- your chosen limit price
- order size
- available balance
- liquidity
- spread
- existing open orders
- exchange fees
- time-in-force settings

And most importantly:

## Double-check the price.

Typing:

**$0.10**

instead of:

**$1.00**

can produce a very different result.

---

# The Key Lesson

A limit order gives you something a market order does not:

## **Control over the price you're willing to accept.**

But that control comes with a cost:

## **Your trade might not happen.**

Remember:

### ⚡ MARKET ORDER

**“Trade now at the best available price.”**

### 🎯 LIMIT ORDER

**“Trade only at my chosen price or better.”**

A limit order controls your execution boundary.

It does not guarantee execution, and it does not guarantee that your trading decision will be profitable.

Understanding that distinction is one of the foundations of becoming a more disciplined trader.
