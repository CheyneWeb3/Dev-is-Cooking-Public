## **Risk Management**

# Stop Loss

---

# What Is a Stop Loss?

A **stop loss** is an order designed to trigger when an asset reaches a predetermined price.

The basic idea is:

> **“If this trade moves too far against me, get me out.”**

Suppose you buy Bitcoin at:

## **$100,000**

You decide that if Bitcoin falls to:

## **$95,000**

you no longer want to remain in the trade.

You could place a stop order around:

## **$95,000**

If the market reaches your stop price, the order becomes active.

What happens next depends on the type of stop order you chose.

---

# Why Traders Use Stop Losses

A stop loss can help traders decide their risk **before** emotions take over.

Without a plan, a trader might think:

> “I'll sell if this drops 5%.”

Then it drops 5%.

They change their mind.

Then it drops 10%.

They tell themselves it will recover.

Then it drops 20%.

This is how a manageable loss can become a much larger one.

A stop loss allows a trader to define a point where they no longer want to remain in the position.

---

# A Simple Example

Imagine you buy a token at:

## **$10**

You decide that you're comfortable risking:

## **$1 per token**

You place your stop around:

## **$9**

If the token falls to that level, your exit order activates.

You have created a basic trading plan:

**Entry:** $10

**Stop:** $9

**Planned risk:** approximately $1 per token

This does not guarantee you'll lose exactly $1 per token.

We'll explain why shortly.

But it gives you a defined point where the trade thesis is considered wrong or the risk becomes unacceptable.

---

# Stop Price vs Execution Price

This is one of the most important concepts in this lesson.

Your:

## **Stop Price**

is usually the price that **activates the order**.

It is not always the exact price you'll receive.

For example:

You own a token trading at:

## $10

Your stop price is:

## $9

The market suddenly falls very quickly:

**$9.20 → $9.05 → $8.70**

Your stop may activate at $9.

But if the next available buyer is only offering $8.70, your actual sale could occur around:

## **$8.70**

This difference is one reason traders need to understand:

## **Slippage**

---

# Stop Losses Do Not Guarantee an Exact Exit Price

A stop loss is often described as protection.

That can be useful shorthand.

But it is important not to misunderstand what that means.

A stop loss does not create guaranteed liquidity.

If nobody is willing to buy at your desired price, your order cannot magically force someone to do so.

In fast-moving or low-liquidity markets, the actual execution can be significantly different from your stop price.

This is particularly important in crypto.

---

# Stop-Market Orders

One common type of stop loss is a:

# Stop-Market Order

It has two stages.

### Stage 1 — Stop Trigger

The market reaches your chosen stop price.

### Stage 2 — Market Order

Your stop activates and becomes a market order.

The priority is then:

## **Get me out of the position.**

For example:

**Entry:** $100

**Stop:** $90

Once the market reaches $90, the system submits a market sell order.

If buyers exist at:

**$89.95**

**$89.80**

**$89.50**

your position could fill across those prices.

---

# Advantage of a Stop-Market Order

The main advantage is:

## **Higher likelihood of execution**

Once triggered, the order attempts to sell using available market liquidity.

This can be useful when exiting the trade matters more than receiving one precise price.

---

# Disadvantage of a Stop-Market Order

The disadvantage is:

## **You don't control the exact execution price.**

In a violent market move, the trade may execute substantially below your stop.

For example:

Stop:

## $10

Sudden price gap:

## $10.20 → $8.50

Your stop may activate, but the available market price could already be around $8.50.

---

# Stop-Limit Orders

Another type is a:

# Stop-Limit Order

This combines two prices:

## Stop Price

The price that activates the order.

## Limit Price

The worst price you're willing to accept.

For example:

**Stop price:** $10

**Limit price:** $9.80

If the token reaches $10, the system activates a limit sell order at $9.80.

That means:

> **“Sell at $9.80 or better.”**

---

# Advantage of a Stop-Limit Order

You have more control over your execution price.

Your order shouldn't simply sell at any available price below your limit.

---

# The Big Risk With Stop-Limit Orders

Your order may:

## **Not execute at all.**

Suppose:

**Stop:** $10

**Limit:** $9.80

The market crashes directly from:

## $10.10 → $9.20

Your stop triggers.

But your sell limit requires:

## $9.80 or better.

If the market is already trading at $9.20, there may be no buyers willing to pay $9.80.

Your position remains open.

This is the central trade-off.

---

# Stop-Market vs Stop-Limit

| Feature | Stop-Market | Stop-Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger price | Yes | Yes |
| Becomes | Market order | Limit order |
| Price control | Low | Higher |
| Execution certainty | Higher | Lower |
| Slippage possible | Yes | Limited by limit price |
| Can remain unfilled | Less likely | Yes |

The choice comes down to what matters more:

## Getting out

or

## Controlling the minimum price

---

# A Stop Loss Is Not the Same as a Limit Sell

This is a very common beginner mistake.

Imagine a token currently trades at:

## **$100**

You want to sell if it falls to:

## **$90**

You cannot simply place a standard sell limit at $90 and expect it to wait.

Why?

A sell limit means:

> **“Sell at $90 or better.”**

Since the market is already trading at $100, your order could execute immediately.

That is why a **stop trigger** exists.

The stop tells the platform:

> **“Do not activate this sell order until the market reaches my trigger price.”**

---

# Why Stop Losses Matter for Position Sizing

A stop loss becomes much more useful when combined with:

## **Position Sizing**

Imagine you are willing to risk:

## **$100**

on one trade.

You buy a token at:

## $10

Your stop is:

## $9

You are risking approximately:

## $1 per token

If you bought 100 tokens:

**100 × $1 = $100 planned risk**

This is a simple example, but it demonstrates something important.

Risk does not come only from deciding where your stop goes.

It also depends on:

## **How large your position is.**

We'll cover position sizing in much more detail later in the series.

---

# A Tight Stop Is Not Automatically Better

Beginners sometimes assume:

> “I'll put my stop extremely close to my entry so I can't lose much.”

That can create another problem.

Normal market movement can trigger your stop even when the larger trend hasn't changed.

Imagine:

**Entry:** $100

**Stop:** $99.90

A tiny amount of normal volatility could close your trade almost immediately.

The market could then continue:

**$99.90 → $105 → $110**

Your stop technically limited the loss.

But it may have been positioned inside ordinary market noise.

---

# A Wide Stop Is Not Automatically Better Either

The opposite mistake is placing the stop extremely far away.

For example:

**Entry:** $100

**Stop:** $50

You may avoid being stopped out by normal volatility.

But now your potential loss per unit is enormous.

The right stop distance depends on:

- strategy
- volatility
- market structure
- position size
- liquidity
- timeframe
- acceptable risk

There is no universal percentage that works for every trade.

---

# Stop Losses and Volatility

Crypto markets are often extremely volatile.

A token might move:

**$10 → $9.50 → $10.20 → $8.90 → $11**

in a short period.

If your stop sits inside that normal volatility, it may trigger frequently.

This is one reason experienced traders consider the characteristics of the asset before choosing a stop level.

A 1% move might be significant for one market and completely normal for another.

---

# Stop Losses in Low-Liquidity Tokens

This is where things can become dangerous.

Imagine a small token trades around:

## **$1**

but there isn't much liquidity.

You place a stop at:

## **$0.90**

If a large seller suddenly hits the market, the available prices might move:

**$1.00**

**$0.95**

**$0.90**

**$0.75**

**$0.60**

very quickly.

Your stop activates at $0.90.

But there may not be enough buyers at $0.90.

A stop-market order could continue selling into significantly lower prices.

This is why:

## **Liquidity matters just as much as the stop price itself.**

---

# Stop Losses on Decentralised Exchanges

Traditional centralised exchanges often manage stop orders through their own matching systems.

Decentralised exchanges can work differently.

A basic automated market maker does not necessarily have a built-in traditional stop-loss system.

Instead, stop functionality may rely on:

- smart contracts
- automation services
- trading protocols
- keepers or executors
- off-chain monitoring
- on-chain trigger logic

The interface might look familiar.

But the mechanism underneath can be very different.

---

# Questions to Ask About On-Chain Stop Orders

If you're using a decentralised trading platform, it can be useful to understand:

- What triggers the stop?
- Who monitors the price?
- Which price source is used?
- Is it an oracle price?
- Is it a liquidity pool price?
- Is execution on-chain?
- Can the order expire?
- Does the platform charge an execution fee?
- What happens if network congestion occurs?
- What happens if liquidity disappears?
- Can the order partially execute?

In decentralised trading, execution mechanics matter.

---

# What Price Actually Triggers the Stop?

Different platforms may use different trigger references.

Examples include:

- last traded price
- mark price
- index price
- bid price
- ask price
- oracle price

This becomes especially important in leveraged trading.

You should understand what your platform means when it says:

## **Trigger Price**

because it may not always be the number you are looking at on the main chart.

---

# Stop Losses and Market Gaps

Markets don't always move smoothly.

Imagine:

**Current price:** $100

**Your stop:** $90

Some major event happens.

The next available market price is:

## **$75**

There may never have been an opportunity to execute around $90.

Your stop can still activate, but your actual exit could be much lower.

This is called:

## **Gap Risk**

Crypto trades 24/7, but rapid price jumps and liquidity gaps can still create similar effects.

---

# Stop Hunting

You'll sometimes hear traders say:

## “They hunted my stop.”

The idea is that price briefly moves into an area where many stop orders are located, triggers those orders, and then reverses.

Large clusters of stop orders can contribute to rapid movements because once triggered they can generate additional market orders.

However, not every price move through your stop is evidence that someone specifically targeted you.

Markets naturally move through areas containing concentrated liquidity.

It is important not to turn every losing trade into a conspiracy theory.

---

# Stops Can Create Cascades

Imagine many traders have stops around:

## **$100**

The market falls to $100.

Those stops begin activating.

Some become market sell orders.

That selling pushes the price lower.

More stops activate.

More selling occurs.

This can contribute to a:

## **Liquidation or Stop Cascade**

In highly leveraged crypto markets, this can happen extremely quickly.

---

# A Stop Loss Does Not Guarantee a Good Trade

This is worth repeating.

A stop loss manages:

## **How you plan to exit when you're wrong.**

It does not tell you:

- whether your entry is good
- whether the token is safe
- whether the trend will continue
- whether the project is legitimate
- whether the market will reverse
- whether the trade will be profitable

A bad trade with a stop loss is still a bad trade.

The stop simply helps define the damage.

---

# Don't Keep Moving the Stop Further Away

One of the classic emotional mistakes looks like this:

**Entry:** $100

**Original stop:** $90

Price reaches $91.

Trader thinks:

> “I'll move it to $85.”

Price reaches $86.

> “Maybe $80.”

Then $75.

The trader technically had a stop-loss plan.

But they kept changing it because they didn't want to accept the loss.

This defeats much of the purpose of defining risk before entering.

---

# Moving a Stop Can Sometimes Be Part of a Strategy

That does not mean a stop should never move.

Some strategies intentionally adjust stops as a trade becomes profitable.

For example:

**Entry:** $100

Price rises to:

## $120

A trader might raise their stop from:

## $90 → $110

The objective is now different.

Instead of limiting the original loss, the trader is attempting to protect part of an existing gain.

This leads us toward another concept:

## **Trailing Stops**

---

# What Is a Trailing Stop?

A trailing stop moves with the market according to predefined rules.

Imagine Bitcoin is:

## $100,000

You use a 5% trailing stop.

As Bitcoin rises, the stop follows behind.

If Bitcoin rises to:

## $110,000

the trailing stop may also move higher.

If Bitcoin then reverses enough to hit the trailing level, an exit is triggered.

The idea is:

## Let profits run while maintaining an exit behind the market.

Trailing stops have their own risks and mechanics, so we'll treat them separately later.

---

# Where Should a Stop Loss Go?

There is no magical answer.

Traders may place stops based on:

- support levels
- previous swing lows
- previous swing highs
- volatility
- percentage risk
- trend structure
- technical invalidation
- maximum acceptable loss

One useful way to think about it is:

> **“At what price would the reason I entered this trade no longer make sense?”**

That can be more meaningful than choosing an arbitrary number.

---

# Percentage-Based Stop Example

Suppose you buy at:

## $100

and choose a:

## 5% stop

Your approximate stop would be:

## $95

Simple.

But the same 5% stop may behave very differently depending on the asset.

For a slow market, 5% could be significant.

For a highly volatile meme token, 5% might be routine movement.

Context matters.

---

# Technical Stop Example

Imagine you buy a token because it has repeatedly held support around:

## $50

Your thinking might be:

> “As long as this support holds, I want to remain in the trade.”

You could place your stop somewhere below the level where you consider that idea invalid.

This is different from simply choosing:

## “I'll risk 5% because 5% sounds reasonable.”

The stop is now connected to the original trade thesis.

---

# Risk-to-Reward

Stop losses also help traders calculate:

## **Risk-to-Reward**

Imagine:

**Entry:** $100

**Stop:** $90

**Target:** $130

Potential risk:

## $10

Potential reward:

## $30

That gives an approximate:

## **1:3 risk-to-reward ratio**

This does not mean the trade will succeed.

It simply allows you to compare what you're risking with what you're attempting to gain.

---

# Stop Loss vs Liquidation

These are not the same thing.

A stop loss is an order you choose.

## Liquidation

can occur when a leveraged position loses enough value that the trading platform forcibly closes it according to its margin rules.

If you're using leverage, waiting for liquidation instead of managing risk can be extremely dangerous.

A stop may be placed before a liquidation level in an attempt to exit earlier.

Again:

## A stop is risk management.

## Liquidation is forced risk resolution.

---

# Common Beginner Stop-Loss Mistakes

## 1. No Stop or Exit Plan

Entering first and deciding what to do only after the trade turns against you.

---

## 2. Stop Too Tight

Normal volatility knocks you out repeatedly.

---

## 3. Stop Too Wide

You risk far more money than intended.

---

## 4. Moving the Stop Further Away

Turning a planned small loss into a much larger one.

---

## 5. Ignoring Liquidity

Assuming the stop price guarantees the execution price.

---

## 6. Using the Same Percentage for Every Asset

Different markets behave differently.

---

## 7. Position Too Large

Even a sensible stop can result in an unacceptable dollar loss if the position is oversized.

---

# Before Placing a Stop Loss

Check:

- your entry price
- stop trigger price
- order type
- limit price if applicable
- position size
- estimated dollar risk
- market liquidity
- normal volatility
- potential slippage
- platform trigger rules

And ask yourself:

## **If this stop executes, am I genuinely comfortable with the loss?**

If the answer is no, the position may be too large.

---

# The Key Lesson

A stop loss is not designed to make a trade profitable.

It is designed to answer a different question:

## **What happens if I'm wrong?**

That question matters enormously in trading.

Remember:

### 🛑 STOP PRICE

**The price that activates your exit order.**

### ⚡ STOP-MARKET

**Prioritises getting out, but the final price can slip.**

### 🎯 STOP-LIMIT

**Gives more price control, but the order might not execute.**

And most importantly:

## **Your stop defines part of your risk. Your position size determines how much that risk actually costs you.**

Good traders don't only think about how much they could make.

They also decide:

## **How much am I prepared to lose if this trade doesn't work?**
