# What Is a Take-Profit Order?

A **take-profit order** is designed to close some or all of a position when the market reaches a predetermined profit level.

In simple terms:

> **“If the price reaches my target, sell some or all of my position.”**

Suppose you buy Bitcoin at:

## **$100,000**

You decide you would like to sell if Bitcoin reaches:

## **$110,000**

You could place a take-profit order around:

## **$110,000**

If the market reaches your target and the necessary liquidity is available, the order can execute automatically.

---

# Why Use a Take-Profit Order?

One of the hardest parts of trading is deciding when to sell.

A trader might buy at:

## $10

The token reaches:

## $15

They think:

> “Maybe it goes to $20.”

It reaches $20.

They think:

> “Maybe $30.”

Then the market reverses.

The token falls:

**$20 → $15 → $11**

The trader had a large unrealised profit but never actually took any of it.

A take-profit order allows a trader to decide:

## **“At this level, I am happy to lock in some profit.”**

before emotions take control.

---

# Profit Is Not Realised Until You Exit

This is an important trading concept.

Imagine you buy:

**1,000 tokens at $1**

Your position costs:

## **$1,000**

The token rises to:

## **$2**

Your position is now displayed as being worth:

## **$2,000**

You have approximately:

## **$1,000 of unrealised profit**

But until you sell, that profit can still change.

If the token drops back to $1, the unrealised gain disappears.

Once you sell, the gain becomes:

## **Realised Profit**

This is why traders often say:

> **“Profit on the screen isn't the same as profit you've actually taken.”**

---

# A Simple Take-Profit Example

Imagine:

**Entry:** $10

You decide:

**Profit target:** $15

You buy:

## **100 tokens**

If the token reaches $15 and your take-profit order executes:

**100 × $15 = $1,500**

Your original cost was:

**100 × $10 = $1,000**

Ignoring fees, your profit would be:

## **$500**

You defined the exit before the market reached it.

---

# Take-Profit Orders Often Use Limit Orders

On many exchanges, a take-profit order ultimately uses a:

## **Limit Order**

For example:

**Current price:** $10

**Take-profit:** $15

Once the necessary conditions are met, the platform attempts to sell your position at $15 or better.

This gives you price control.

But remember what we learned about limit orders:

## **Execution is not guaranteed.**

If there is not enough liquidity at your target, the order can be partially filled or remain unfilled.

---

# Take-Profit vs Sell Limit

A normal sell limit can often be used as a basic take-profit order.

Suppose:

**Current token price:** $10

You own:

**100 tokens**

You want to sell at:

## **$15**

You can place:

**Sell 100 tokens at $15**

The order sits waiting.

If buyers become willing to pay $15 or more, your order can execute.

In that situation, the sell limit effectively acts as your:

## **Take-Profit Order**

---

# Triggered Take-Profit Orders

Some trading platforms offer more advanced take-profit orders that contain:

## A Trigger Price

and sometimes:

## A Limit Price

For example:

**Trigger:** $15

**Limit:** $14.95

The market reaching $15 activates the order.

The platform then attempts to sell at:

## $14.95 or better

This allows more control over when the order becomes active.

The exact mechanics vary between platforms, so it is worth understanding how your exchange handles take-profit orders.

---

# Taking Profit Doesn't Mean Selling Everything

A common beginner assumption is:

> “Taking profit means closing the whole trade.”

It doesn't have to.

You can sell:

- 10%
- 25%
- 50%
- 75%
- or any other portion

while leaving the rest of the position open.

This is known as:

# **Scaling Out**

---

# Example of Scaling Out

Imagine you buy:

## **1,000 tokens at $1**

Instead of choosing one exit price, you create several targets.

### Target 1

Sell 250 tokens at:

## $1.50

### Target 2

Sell 250 tokens at:

## $2.00

### Target 3

Sell 250 tokens at:

## $2.50

### Target 4

Keep the final 250 tokens open.

This lets you realise profit gradually while maintaining some exposure if the market continues rising.

---

# Why Traders Take Partial Profits

Partial profit taking can help with several things.

## It Reduces Exposure

Once part of the position has been sold, less capital remains exposed to a market reversal.

## It Locks In Some Gains

Even if the remaining position later falls, some profit has already been realised.

## It Reduces Emotional Pressure

A trader who has already taken profit may find it easier to manage the remaining position.

## It Allows Continued Upside

You don't have to choose between:

**sell everything**

and

**sell nothing**

---

# The Trade-Off With Taking Profit Early

There is also a downside.

Imagine:

**Entry:** $1

You sell everything at:

## $1.50

Then the token continues:

**$2**

**$5**

**$10**

You made a profit.

But you also gave up the additional upside.

This does not mean your take-profit decision was wrong.

Trading decisions should be judged based on your plan and risk — not only on what the market did afterwards.

No trader consistently sells at the exact top.

---

# Trying to Sell the Exact Top Is Usually Unrealistic

Beginners often look back at a chart and think:

> “I should have sold right there.”

The highest point is obvious after it has already happened.

In real time, nobody knows with certainty where the top will be.

A token at:

## $20

might reverse to $10.

Or continue to:

## $30

## $50

## $100

A take-profit strategy is not about magically finding the exact top.

It is about defining prices where you are satisfied to realise gains.

---

# Take Profit and Risk-to-Reward

Take-profit levels are often considered alongside:

## **Stop Losses**

Suppose:

**Entry:** $100

**Stop:** $90

**Take-profit:** $120

Your potential risk is:

## $10

Your potential reward is:

## $20

That gives an approximate:

# **1:2 Risk-to-Reward Ratio**

You are risking $1 for every $2 of potential reward.

This does not tell you whether the trade will succeed.

But it gives you a framework for comparing:

## **What you are risking**

with:

## **What you are trying to make**

---

# Risk-to-Reward Example

Imagine two possible trades.

## Trade A

**Entry:** $100  
**Stop:** $95  
**Target:** $105

Risk:

## $5

Potential reward:

## $5

Risk-to-reward:

## **1:1**

---

## Trade B

**Entry:** $100  
**Stop:** $95  
**Target:** $115

Risk:

## $5

Potential reward:

## $15

Risk-to-reward:

## **1:3**

Trade B offers more potential reward relative to the planned risk.

That does not automatically make it the better trade.

A target at $115 might also be much less likely to be reached.

Risk-to-reward is one ingredient — not the entire recipe.

---

# Combining a Stop Loss and Take Profit

A trader can define both sides of a trade before entering.

For example:

## Entry

$100

## Stop Loss

$90

## Take Profit

$130

The trader now knows:

- where they plan to enter
- where they will exit if wrong
- where they plan to take profit if right

This creates a much more structured trade than:

> “I'll buy now and figure it out later.”

---

# Bracket Orders

Some platforms allow a structure commonly known as a:

# **Bracket Order**

A bracket can contain:

- an entry
- a stop loss
- a take-profit order

Once the trade is open, the position is effectively surrounded by two exit conditions.

If the take profit executes, the stop may be cancelled.

If the stop executes, the take-profit may be cancelled.

Platforms implement these differently, so always check the actual order behaviour.

---

# OCO Orders — One Cancels the Other

You may also encounter:

# **OCO — One Cancels the Other**

Imagine you hold a token at:

## $100

You create:

**Take Profit:** $120

and

**Stop Loss:** $90

If the price reaches $120 and your take profit executes, the $90 stop order is cancelled.

If the market falls to $90 and the stop executes, the $120 take-profit order is cancelled.

The purpose is to prevent both exit orders from remaining active after the position has already closed.

---

# Why This Matters

Imagine you sold your entire position at your take-profit target.

But an old stop-loss sell order remained active.

If you later bought the token again and that old order was still valid, it could potentially interfere with your new position depending on the platform.

Understanding how linked orders behave matters.

---

# Take-Profit Orders Can Partially Fill

Just like limit orders, a take-profit order can receive a:

## **Partial Fill**

Suppose you want to sell:

## 10,000 tokens at $2

but buyers only want:

## 2,000 tokens

at that price.

You might sell 2,000 tokens while the remaining:

## 8,000

stay open.

The market could then fall before the rest of your order executes.

This is another reason liquidity matters.

---

# Liquidity Still Matters When Taking Profit

Imagine a small token is showing:

## $5

You own:

## 100,000 tokens

The value displayed might make your position look extremely profitable.

But there may only be enough liquidity to sell:

## 1,000 tokens around $5

Attempting to sell your entire position could move the market substantially.

This is why:

## **Displayed token price does not automatically equal the price at which your whole position can be sold.**

Your real exit depends on available liquidity.

---

# Taking Profit on a DEX

On a decentralised exchange, take-profit functionality can work differently from a centralised order book.

A normal AMM swap executes against:

## **Liquidity Pools**

More advanced DeFi trading systems may provide take-profit orders through:

- smart contracts
- automation protocols
- on-chain limit orders
- off-chain execution services
- keepers
- price oracles
- conditional trading systems

The interface might simply display:

## “Take Profit”

but under the hood there may be several components involved.

---

# Questions to Ask About DEX Take-Profit Orders

Before relying on one, it can be useful to know:

- What price triggers the order?
- What price source is used?
- Does it use an oracle?
- Does it monitor a liquidity pool?
- Who executes the transaction?
- Does the order expire?
- Are there execution fees?
- Can the order partially fill?
- What happens during network congestion?
- What happens if liquidity disappears?

Understanding the execution mechanics becomes especially important when automated orders are placed on-chain.

---

# Take-Profit Orders and Fast Markets

A market can move through a target extremely quickly.

Suppose:

**Take-profit target:** $10

The market moves:

**$9.80 → $10.10 → $9.50**

Your order may execute around the target if sufficient liquidity exists.

But if your order depends on a particular trigger or limit structure, it may behave differently.

This is why order configuration matters.

---

# Greed Is One of the Biggest Problems With Taking Profit

Imagine you enter at:

## $1

Your original target is:

## $2

Price reaches $2.

Instead of taking profit, you think:

> “I'll wait for $3.”

It reaches $3.

Now:

> “Definitely going to $5.”

Then the market reverses:

**$3 → $2 → $1.20**

The problem wasn't necessarily the market.

The trader abandoned the original plan because expectations kept changing.

Taking profit requires discipline just like cutting a loss.

---

# Fear Can Cause the Opposite Problem

Some traders sell too quickly.

They buy at:

## $10

Price rises to:

## $10.20

They immediately sell because they are scared the profit will disappear.

The market then continues to:

## $15

This is why a predefined trading plan can be helpful.

Without one, decisions may be driven almost entirely by:

## **Fear and greed**

---

# Taking Your Initial Investment Out

One strategy sometimes used by traders is to recover their original capital after a position has increased substantially.

For example:

You invest:

## $1,000

Your position grows to:

## $2,000

You sell:

## $1,000 worth

Your original capital has now been removed from the position.

The remaining $1,000 stays invested.

People sometimes describe this remaining amount as:

> “House money.”

But that phrase can be misleading.

The remaining tokens still have real value.

Losing them is still losing capital.

---

# Do Not Treat Unrealised Profit as Free Money

Imagine:

Original investment:

## $1,000

Position increases to:

## $10,000

You might think:

> “I'm only risking the original $1,000.”

That isn't true anymore.

At that point you control:

## $10,000 of value

If the position collapses to zero, the economic loss from the peak is substantial.

This is why taking profit can become increasingly important as positions grow.

---

# Take-Profit Targets Based on Percentages

One simple method is percentage targets.

For example:

**Entry:** $100

### Target 1

+10% = $110

### Target 2

+20% = $120

### Target 3

+50% = $150

Percentage targets are easy to understand.

But they don't account for things such as:

- market structure
- support
- resistance
- volatility
- liquidity

They are simply predetermined levels.

---

# Take-Profit Targets Based on Resistance

A trader may instead look at previous market structure.

Imagine a token repeatedly struggled to move above:

## $20

You enter at:

## $15

You might decide to take some profit near the previous resistance area.

The reasoning is:

> “This is an area where sellers have previously appeared.”

Again, there is no guarantee the market will behave the same way.

---

# Taking Profit at Multiple Targets

A more structured example might look like:

## Entry

$10

## Stop Loss

$8

## Target 1

$12 — sell 25%

## Target 2

$15 — sell 25%

## Target 3

$20 — sell 25%

## Remaining Position

25% left open

This lets the trader gradually reduce exposure as the price moves in their favour.

---

# Moving the Stop After Taking Profit

Some traders combine partial profit taking with stop adjustments.

For example:

**Entry:** $10

Price reaches:

## $15

The trader sells part of the position.

They may then move their stop on the remaining position from:

## $8

to:

## $10

or another chosen level.

The objective is to reduce the amount of original capital still at risk.

This is sometimes described as moving the stop to:

## **Break-Even**

---

# What Does Break-Even Mean?

If you bought at:

## $10

and later moved your stop to:

## $10

you might describe that as a break-even stop.

But remember:

Trading fees, spreads, funding costs and slippage can mean your true break-even price is not exactly your entry price.

So:

## Entry price ≠ always true break-even price.

---

# Should You Always Move a Stop to Break-Even?

No.

Doing so can sometimes protect capital.

But it can also cause a trade to close during ordinary volatility before continuing higher.

Like most trading tools, it depends on the strategy.

There is no rule saying every profitable trade should immediately have its stop moved to break-even.

---

# Take Profit vs Trailing Stop

A take-profit order usually defines a fixed target.

For example:

## Sell at $150

A trailing stop behaves differently.

Instead of selling at one fixed upside target, it follows the market higher and attempts to exit after a reversal.

For example:

The token rises:

**$100 → $120 → $150 → $180**

A trailing stop may continue moving upward behind the price.

If the market then falls enough, the trailing stop activates.

This can allow more upside while still providing an exit condition.

But trailing stops introduce their own risks and behaviour.

---

# Taking Profit in Leveraged Trading

Take-profit orders become particularly important when leverage is involved.

Leverage magnifies:

## Gains

and

## Losses

If a trader opens a leveraged position without planning exits, the position can change extremely quickly.

Take-profit and stop-loss orders are commonly used together to define:

## **Potential upside**

and

## **Maximum planned downside**

before the trade begins.

But neither order eliminates the underlying risks of leverage.

---

# Profit Targets Should Match the Trade

Different trades may require different expectations.

A trader might hold:

## Bitcoin

for a much longer period than a highly speculative small-cap token.

A 5% move could be meaningful in one strategy and irrelevant in another.

Profit targets may depend on:

- timeframe
- asset volatility
- market structure
- liquidity
- trading strategy
- position size
- expected risk
- broader market conditions

There is no universal:

## “Always take profit at 20%.”

---

# Common Beginner Take-Profit Mistakes

## 1. Having No Profit Plan

Knowing how to buy but having no idea when to sell.

---

## 2. Constantly Moving the Target Higher

The target gets reached, but greed prevents execution.

---

## 3. Selling Everything Too Early

Closing the entire position after a tiny move without considering the original plan.

---

## 4. Waiting for the Exact Top

Trying to perfectly predict something that is only obvious afterwards.

---

## 5. Ignoring Liquidity

Assuming an entire position can be sold at the displayed market price.

---

## 6. Forgetting About Fees

A small apparent profit can disappear after trading costs.

---

## 7. Using Targets Without Considering Risk

A large potential reward means little if the downside is even larger.

---

# Before Setting a Take-Profit Order

Check:

- entry price
- target price
- position size
- percentage being sold
- remaining position
- expected profit
- trading fees
- liquidity
- order type
- potential partial fills
- stop-loss location
- risk-to-reward

And ask yourself:

## **If the market reaches this price, will I actually be satisfied taking profit here?**

If the answer is yes, then changing the target purely because price is pumping may be emotion rather than strategy.

# The Key Lesson

A take-profit order helps answer:

## **“What will I do if this trade goes right?”**

A stop loss answers:

## **“What will I do if this trade goes wrong?”**

Together they can form the foundations of a trading plan.

Remember:

### 💰 TAKE PROFIT

**An order designed to realise gains at a predetermined level.**

### 🧩 PARTIAL PROFIT

**Sell part of the position while keeping the remainder open.**

### ⚖️ RISK-TO-REWARD

**Compare how much you are prepared to lose with how much you are attempting to gain.**

And most importantly:

## **A profitable trade can become an unprofitable trade if you never actually realise the profit.**

Taking profit isn't about perfectly predicting the top.

It is about deciding what outcome is acceptable to you and following a plan.
