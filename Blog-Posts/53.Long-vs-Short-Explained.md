## Buy low.

## Sell higher.

That is called:

# **Going Long**

That is called:

# **Going Short**

Understanding the difference between long and short positions is essential before moving into futures, perpetuals, margin trading or leverage.

So let's cook it down.

---

# What Does “Long” Mean?

When a trader goes:

## **Long**

they are taking a position that benefits if the asset's price rises.

In simple terms:

> **“I think the price is going up.”**

Suppose Bitcoin is trading at:

## **$100,000**

You buy Bitcoin because you believe it could rise to:

## **$110,000**

If the price increases, your position gains value.

If the price falls, your position loses value.

---

# A Simple Long Example

Imagine you buy:

## **1 token at $100**

The price rises to:

## **$120**

Ignoring fees, your gain is:

## **$20**

You bought at $100 and could sell at $120.

That is a:

# **Long Position**

---

# What Happens If the Price Falls?

If you buy the same token at:

## $100

and it falls to:

## $80

your position has lost:

## $20

You are still long.

But the market moved in the opposite direction from what you expected.

So the basic relationship is:

### Long Position

**Price rises = profit**

**Price falls = loss**

---

# What Does “Short” Mean?

A:

## **Short Position**

is designed to benefit when the price of an asset falls.

In simple terms:

> **“I think the price is going down.”**

For example, Bitcoin is trading at:

## $100,000

You believe the price will fall to:

## $90,000

Instead of buying Bitcoin, you open a short position.

If Bitcoin falls, the short can gain value.

If Bitcoin rises, the short loses value.

---

# A Simple Short Example

Imagine a token trades at:

## **$100**

You open a short position equivalent to:

## **1 token**

The price falls to:

## **$80**

The difference is:

## **$20**

Ignoring fees and other trading costs, the short position has gained approximately $20.

Why?

Because you effectively entered the position at $100 and closed it at a lower price.

---

# Long vs Short at a Glance

| Position | You Expect | Profit If | Loss If |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long | Price rises | Price goes up | Price goes down |
| Short | Price falls | Price goes down | Price goes up |

A very simple way to remember it is:

## 📈 LONG = bullish

## 📉 SHORT = bearish

---

# What Does Bullish Mean?

If someone is:

## **Bullish**

they generally believe an asset or market is likely to rise.

For example:

> “I'm bullish on Bitcoin.”

means:

> “I expect Bitcoin to increase in value.”

---

# What Does Bearish Mean?

If someone is:

## **Bearish**

they generally expect the market or asset to fall.

For example:

> “I'm bearish on ETH this week.”

means:

> “I think ETH may decline.”

These words describe market expectations.

They don't necessarily mean the person already has an open trade.

# How Does Short Selling Actually Work?

This is where things become more interesting.

In traditional markets, one way to short an asset involves:

1. Borrowing the asset
2. Selling it
3. Buying it back later
4. Returning the borrowed asset

Suppose you borrow:

## 1 token

when it is worth:

## $100

You sell it for $100.

Later the token falls to:

## $70

You buy one token back for $70.

You return the borrowed token.

Ignoring fees, the difference is:

## $30 profit

That is the traditional logic behind short selling.

---

# Why Would Anyone Lend You the Asset?

In margin markets, the exchange or another market participant may provide assets for borrowing.

The borrower may pay:

## Interest

or other borrowing fees.

The exact system depends on the platform.

In crypto, short exposure is also commonly created through:

- futures
- perpetual contracts
- margin trading
- derivatives
- decentralised lending protocols

You may not physically borrow and sell a token yourself.

The platform may represent the short through a contract instead.

---

# What Are Futures?

A:

## **Futures Contract**

is a financial contract based on the price of an underlying asset.

Instead of necessarily buying the actual Bitcoin or token, traders can trade a contract whose value moves with the underlying market.

Futures can generally be used to take either:

## Long positions

or

## Short positions

This makes them useful for traders who want exposure in either market direction.

---

# What Are Perpetual Futures?

Crypto traders frequently use:

## **Perpetual Futures**

or simply:

## **Perps**

These resemble futures contracts but normally do not have a traditional expiration date.

You can remain in the position while the platform's margin and funding requirements are satisfied.

Perpetuals have become one of the most common ways crypto traders go short.

---

# Spot Trading Is Different

If you use a normal spot exchange and buy:

## 1 BTC

you actually own that BTC balance on the platform.

That is:

# **Spot Trading**

A typical spot trade is naturally long.

You buy an asset and hope its value increases.

To short, you usually need something additional such as:

- margin borrowing
- futures
- perpetuals
- another derivative

---

# Long Doesn't Automatically Mean Spot

This distinction is important.

You can also:

## Go long using futures.

For example, instead of buying actual Bitcoin, you could open a Bitcoin perpetual long.

Your position benefits if Bitcoin rises.

But you may not own any actual Bitcoin.

So:

## Long = direction

while:

## Spot/Futures = type of market

They describe different things.

---

# Opening and Closing a Long Position

Suppose BTC trades at:

## $100,000

You open a long.

Later BTC reaches:

## $110,000

You close the position.

Your position benefited from a:

## $10,000 upward move

If BTC instead fell to:

## $90,000

your position would have moved:

## $10,000 against you

The actual dollar profit or loss depends on the size of your position.

---

# Opening and Closing a Short Position

Now suppose BTC trades at:

## $100,000

You open a short.

BTC falls to:

## $90,000

The market moved:

## $10,000 in your favour

If BTC instead rises to:

## $110,000

the market moved:

## $10,000 against the short.

Again, your actual profit or loss depends on position size.

# Long and Short P&L Example

Let's simplify things using one token.

## Long Trade

Entry:

## $100

Exit:

## $120

Difference:

## +$20

---

## Short Trade

Entry:

## $100

Exit:

## $80

Difference:

## +$20

Both traders made money.

But they profited from opposite directions.

# Can Long and Short Traders Both Make Money?

Yes — just not necessarily from the same price movement at the same time.

Different traders:

- enter at different prices
- exit at different prices
- use different timeframes
- manage risk differently

One trader may be long for several months.

Another may short a two-hour market correction.

Their strategies do not have to contradict each other.

---

# Timeframe Matters

Someone can say:

> “I'm bullish on Bitcoin long term but bearish today.”

That can make perfect sense.

They might believe Bitcoin will eventually rise significantly but expect a temporary decline first.

So market direction always needs context.

Are we talking about:

- minutes?
- hours?
- days?
- months?
- years?

---

# Why Short Positions Are Different From Simply Not Buying

If you think a token will fall, you could simply:

## Not buy it.

Your profit:

## $0

Your loss:

## $0

A short position is different.

You actively take financial exposure to the downside.

If the asset falls, you may profit.

If it rises, you may lose.

---

# The Special Risk of Short Selling

A long and short position do not have perfectly symmetrical risk.

Let's see why.

Suppose you buy a token at:

## $100

The absolute lowest its price can theoretically fall is:

## $0

So if you own one token, your maximum loss is:

## $100

assuming no leverage and ignoring other costs.

You cannot lose more value from that token falling once it reaches zero.

---

# A Short Has No Similar Upper Price Limit

Now imagine you short the same token at:

## $100

What is the highest price it can theoretically reach?

There is no fixed upper limit.

It could rise to:

- $200
- $500
- $1,000
- $10,000

That means an unhedged short position can theoretically have:

# **Unlimited Loss Potential**

This is one of the most important differences between long and short positions.

---

# Short Squeeze Explained

A:

## **Short Squeeze**

can happen when an asset rises sharply and traders who are short begin closing their positions.

To close many short positions, traders may need to buy.

That buying creates even more upward pressure.

More shorts begin losing money.

More positions close.

More buying occurs.

The cycle can accelerate rapidly.

---

# Simple Short Squeeze Example

Imagine a token is:

## $10

Many traders short it.

Then strong buying pushes it to:

## $12

Some shorts exit.

Their buy orders help push it toward:

## $15

More shorts are forced out.

Price jumps to:

## $20

The rising market itself creates pressure on traders betting against it.

That is a simplified version of a short squeeze.

---

# What Is a Long Squeeze?

The opposite can happen too.

If many traders are heavily long and price suddenly falls, leveraged long positions may begin closing or being liquidated.

That selling can push the market lower.

More longs are forced out.

This is sometimes called a:

## **Long Squeeze**

or liquidation cascade.

---

# Enter Leverage

Long and short positions become much more dangerous when traders use:

# **Leverage**

Leverage allows a trader to control a position larger than the amount of capital they provide.

For example:

You have:

## $1,000

Using:

## 10× leverage

you may control approximately:

## $10,000

of market exposure.

This magnifies both:

## Profits

and

## Losses

---

# A Simple Leveraged Long Example

You deposit:

## $1,000

and open:

## $10,000 long exposure

If the market rises:

## 5%

the position moves approximately:

## $500

in your favour before costs.

That is a:

## 50% gain

relative to your original $1,000 margin.

Sounds attractive.

But the same magnification works against you.

A:

## 5% fall

could mean approximately:

## $500 loss

before fees and platform-specific mechanics.

---

# A Leveraged Short Works the Same Way in Reverse

If you short:

## $10,000

with $1,000 margin and the market falls:

## 5%

you may gain approximately:

## $500

before costs.

If the market rises:

## 5%

you may lose approximately:

## $500

Leverage does not care whether you are long or short.

It amplifies exposure in both directions.

---

# What Is Liquidation?

When trading with leverage, your position usually has a:

## **Liquidation Price**

If losses become too large relative to your available collateral, the platform may forcibly close the position.

This protects the trading system from allowing your account to fall too far below its margin requirements.

Liquidation can happen to:

## Long positions

and

## Short positions

---

# Long Liquidation

You open a leveraged:

## Long

The market falls substantially.

Your collateral can no longer safely support the position.

The platform closes it.

---

# Short Liquidation

You open a leveraged:

## Short

The market rises substantially.

Your losses consume too much of the available collateral.

The platform liquidates the position.

The direction changes.

The concept does not.

---

# Liquidation Is Not a Stop Loss

These should never be confused.

## Stop Loss

A risk-management order chosen by the trader.

## Liquidation

A forced closure by the trading system because margin requirements are no longer being met.

Waiting for liquidation is generally not the same as having a risk-management plan.

---

# Cross Margin vs Isolated Margin

Leveraged trading platforms may offer:

## **Isolated Margin**

and:

## **Cross Margin**

### Isolated Margin

A specific amount of collateral is assigned to one position.

The risk is more contained to that position.

### Cross Margin

More of your account's available collateral may support the position.

This can move liquidation farther away, but it may expose more of your account balance.

Different exchanges implement these systems differently, so traders should understand the platform rules before using them.

---

# Funding Rates

Crypto perpetual futures often use:

## **Funding Payments**

to help keep perpetual prices close to the underlying spot market.

Depending on market conditions:

- longs may pay shorts
- shorts may pay longs

Funding can affect the cost of maintaining a position over time.

A trade can move in your expected direction and still incur funding costs.

---

# A Positive Funding Rate

In many perpetual systems, positive funding commonly means:

## Longs pay shorts.

This often occurs when perpetual futures are trading above the spot market and bullish demand is strong.

---

# A Negative Funding Rate

Negative funding commonly means:

## Shorts pay longs.

This can happen when bearish positioning dominates and perpetual prices trade below the underlying spot market.

Exact calculations vary by platform.

---

# Why Funding Matters

Imagine you open a long expecting to hold it for several weeks.

If funding is expensive, you could repeatedly pay funding while the position remains open.

Those costs reduce the final profit.

The same issue can affect short positions when funding works in the opposite direction.

So profit and loss isn't always simply:

## Entry Price → Exit Price

Other costs can matter too.
