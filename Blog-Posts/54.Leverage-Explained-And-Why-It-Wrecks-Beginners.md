# **Leverage**

Leverage is one of the most attractive tools in trading because it allows a trader to control a larger position with less capital.

It is also one of the fastest ways for an inexperienced trader to lose money.

Why?

Because leverage does not just multiply profits.

## It multiplies exposure.

And that means losses become larger too.

So let's cook it down.

---

# What Is Leverage?

Leverage allows you to control a position larger than the amount of money you put up yourself.

For example, imagine you have:

## **$1,000**

Without leverage, you could open approximately:

## **$1,000 of market exposure**

But with:

## **5× leverage**

you may control approximately:

## **$5,000 of exposure**

With:

## **10× leverage**

you may control approximately:

## **$10,000**

Your $1,000 is effectively being used as collateral or margin supporting the larger position.

---

# Leverage Does Not Create Free Money

This is the first mistake to remove.

If you have:

## $1,000

and use:

## 10× leverage

you do not suddenly own $10,000 of free money.

You have created:

## **$10,000 of market exposure**

supported by your:

## **$1,000 of collateral**

If the market moves in your favour, your gains can be much larger relative to your deposited capital.

If the market moves against you, the exact same thing happens to your losses.

---

# A Simple 2× Leverage Example

Suppose you have:

## $1,000

You use:

## 2× leverage

Your position size becomes:

## $2,000

If the market rises:

## 5%

your position gains approximately:

## $100

Because:

**5% of $2,000 = $100**

Relative to your original $1,000 collateral, that is approximately:

## **10%**

before fees and other costs.

Without leverage, the same 5% move on $1,000 would only produce:

## $50

So leverage increased the result.

---

# The Same Example When Price Goes Against You

Now imagine the market falls:

## 5%

Your $2,000 position loses approximately:

## $100

Relative to your $1,000 collateral, that is:

## **-10%**

The leverage magnified the loss just as easily as it magnified the gain.

That is the core rule:

# **Leverage amplifies both directions.**

---

# What Does 5× Leverage Mean?

At:

## **5× leverage**

every 1% market move produces roughly a 5% change relative to your margin, before fees and platform mechanics.

For example:

### Market rises 1%

Approximate impact:

## +5%

### Market falls 1%

Approximate impact:

## -5%

A relatively small price movement now has a much larger effect on your capital.

---

# What Does 10× Leverage Mean?

At:

## **10× leverage**

a 1% move in the asset creates roughly a:

## 10%

change relative to the margin supporting the position.

A:

## 5% adverse move

could therefore represent roughly:

## 50%

of your margin.

A movement that might be normal volatility in crypto can become a major event for a highly leveraged position.

---

# What About 100× Leverage?

This is where things become extreme.

At:

## **100× leverage**

you are controlling roughly:

## $100 of exposure for every $1 of margin.

That means an adverse move of around:

## 1%

can become catastrophic.

The exact liquidation level depends on fees, maintenance margin and exchange rules, so it is not as simple as saying exactly 1%.

But the important point is:

## At very high leverage, your position may have almost no room to breathe.

Normal market volatility can be enough to wipe it out.

---

# Leverage Shrinks Your Margin for Error

Think of leverage like reducing the space between your trade and disaster.

Suppose Bitcoin regularly moves:

## 2%–5%

during normal market activity.

If you are trading without leverage, those moves may be manageable.

If you're using:

## 50× leverage

a relatively small move against you can threaten the entire position.

The market does not need to crash.

It only needs to move normally.

---

# What Is Margin?

The money supporting a leveraged position is called:

# **Margin**

Suppose you open:

## $10,000

of exposure using:

## 10× leverage.

You may only need around:

## $1,000

of initial margin.

That $1,000 helps cover losses if the position moves against you.

---

# Initial Margin

## **Initial Margin**

is the amount required to open the position.

For example:

Position size:

## $10,000

Leverage:

## 10×

Approximate initial margin:

## $1,000

This is simplified, because exchanges may also account for fees and other requirements.

---

# Maintenance Margin

Once the position is open, the exchange usually requires a minimum amount of equity to remain in the position.

This is called:

## **Maintenance Margin**

If your position loses too much value and falls below this requirement, the exchange may begin liquidation.

---

# What Is Liquidation?

Liquidation is when the platform forcibly closes a leveraged position because there is no longer enough collateral supporting it.

This can happen because the market moves too far against you.

For example:

You open a highly leveraged long.

The market falls.

Your losses consume too much of your available margin.

Eventually the exchange decides:

> **“There is no longer enough collateral to safely keep this position open.”**

The position is forcibly closed.

That is:

# **Liquidation**

---

# Liquidation Is Not the Same as a Stop Loss

This distinction is critical.

## Stop Loss

A planned order chosen by the trader.

## Liquidation

A forced closure imposed by the trading platform.

A trader using leverage should generally understand their liquidation price before entering.

Waiting for liquidation is not the same as having a risk-management plan.

---

# Simple Leveraged Long Example

Imagine Bitcoin is:

## $100,000

You use:

## $1,000 margin

with:

## 10× leverage

to create roughly:

## $10,000 of long exposure.

Bitcoin rises:

## 5%

Your position gains approximately:

## $500

before fees and funding.

That is around:

## +50%

relative to your $1,000 margin.

Looks fantastic.

Now reverse the move.

Bitcoin falls:

## 5%

Your position loses approximately:

## $500

That is:

## -50%

relative to your margin.

Same leverage.

Same market move.

Opposite result.

---

# Simple Leveraged Short Example

Leverage works in the opposite direction for a short.

Suppose you open:

## $10,000 short exposure

using:

## $1,000 margin.

The asset falls:

## 5%

Approximate gain:

## $500

But if the asset rises:

## 5%

Approximate loss:

## $500

Leverage does not care whether you're bullish or bearish.

It magnifies your exposure either way.

---

# Why Beginners Love Leverage

Leverage is attractive because people immediately see the upside.

They think:

> “If Bitcoin rises 5%, I could make 50% with 10× leverage.”

That calculation may be roughly correct before costs.

But the beginner often forgets the other half:

> **“If Bitcoin falls 5%, I can also lose roughly 50% of my margin.”**

People naturally focus on the potential reward.

Leverage punishes anyone who ignores the potential loss.

---

# Why Leverage Wrecks Beginners

There are several common reasons.

---

# 1. Beginners Use Too Much Leverage

A trader with $1,000 sees the ability to control:

## $10,000

## $25,000

or even:

## $100,000

of exposure.

That feels powerful.

But the position becomes far too large relative to the trader's actual capital.

A small adverse movement creates enormous losses.

---

# 2. They Confuse Leverage With Position Size

These are related, but they are not the same thing.

Suppose you have:

## $10,000

in your account.

You could use:

## 10× leverage

but only open:

## $1,000

of market exposure.

That would still be a relatively small position compared with your total capital.

Alternatively, you could use all your available margin and create an enormous leveraged position.

The danger is not just the number shown beside leverage.

The actual risk depends heavily on:

## **Position size**

---

# Position Size Matters More Than the Button

Imagine Trader A has:

## $10,000

and opens a:

## $1,000 position

using leverage.

Trader B has:

## $1,000

and opens:

## $10,000 exposure

using leverage.

Both may technically be using leverage.

But their risk is completely different.

Always think in terms of:

# **Total exposure + available capital + stop distance**

not just:

# **“I'm using 10×.”**

---

# 3. Beginners Don't Understand Liquidation

A beginner sees:

## 20× leverage

and thinks:

> “I'll just close the position if it starts going badly.”

But highly leveraged positions can deteriorate extremely quickly.

Crypto trades continuously.

Price can move violently.

By the time the trader reacts, the position may already be close to liquidation.

---

# 4. They Put the Liquidation Price Where the Stop Should Be

A trader may think:

> “I'm fine because liquidation is 5% away.”

But a 5% move in crypto may be completely normal.

The better question is not:

## “How far away is liquidation?”

It is:

## **“At what price is my trade idea wrong?”**

The stop should generally relate to the trading plan.

Leverage should then be sized so the position can survive normal movement without risking an unacceptable loss.

---

# 5. They Trade With No Stop Loss

High leverage and no stop can be a disastrous combination.

Without a planned exit, the position can rapidly move toward liquidation.

The trader then starts hoping.

Hope becomes panic.

Panic becomes:

## Forced liquidation.

A stop loss cannot remove all risk, but entering a leveraged trade without understanding your exit is extremely dangerous.

---

# 6. They Chase Losses

Imagine a trader loses:

## $100

They think:

> “I'll use more leverage and make it back.”

They lose another:

## $200

Then increase leverage again.

This is known as:

## **Revenge Trading**

Leverage makes revenge trading especially dangerous because each emotional decision can dramatically increase exposure.

---

# 7. They Mistake Luck for Skill

A beginner opens:

## 50× leverage

The market moves 1% in their favour.

They make a huge percentage return.

They think:

> “I'm good at this.”

Then they repeat the trade with more money.

Eventually the market moves 1% the other way.

The earlier success may have been mostly:

## **Leverage + luck**

rather than trading skill.

This is one of the most dangerous lessons the market can teach a beginner.

Winning immediately can sometimes create worse habits than losing immediately.

---

# Leverage Changes Normal Volatility Into Account Volatility

Imagine an asset moves:

## 2%

That sounds small.

With no leverage:

## 2% market movement ≈ 2% position movement.

At:

## 5× leverage

the effect relative to margin may be roughly:

## 10%

At:

## 10×

roughly:

## 20%

At:

## 25×

roughly:

## 50%

At:

## 50×

roughly:

## 100%

These are simplified illustrations before fees, maintenance margin and liquidation mechanics.

But they show why leverage matters.

---

# Higher Leverage Does Not Make the Trade Better

Suppose your trading idea is:

> “Bitcoin will rise from $100,000 to $105,000.”

Using:

## 2×

## 10×

or

## 50×

does not make that prediction more accurate.

The market setup remains exactly the same.

Leverage only changes:

## **How strongly the result affects your capital.**

It amplifies your exposure.

It does not improve your analysis.

---

# Leverage Does Not Improve Your Win Rate

This is another important point.

If your strategy wins:

## 50% of the time

adding leverage does not automatically make it win:

## 70% of the time.

Your win rate remains tied to the strategy.

Leverage simply makes:

- winners larger
- losers larger
- mistakes more expensive

---

# Cross Margin Explained

Many derivatives platforms offer:

## **Cross Margin**

With cross margin, more of your account balance may be available to support your open position.

For example:

Account balance:

## $10,000

Position margin:

## $1,000

If the position moves against you, the platform may use additional available account equity to prevent immediate liquidation.

This can move the liquidation price farther away.

But it also means:

## More of your account may be exposed.

---

# Isolated Margin Explained

With:

## **Isolated Margin**

a specific amount of collateral is assigned to the position.

For example:

You assign:

## $1,000

to one trade.

If that position fails, the risk is more contained to that isolated margin according to the platform's rules.

The rest of your account is not automatically used in the same way to support the position.

---

# Cross vs Isolated

A simplified comparison:

| Feature | Isolated Margin | Cross Margin |
|---|---|---|
| Margin assigned | Specific position | Shared account balance |
| Risk containment | Higher | Lower |
| Liquidation distance | Often closer | Can be farther |
| More account capital exposed? | Usually less | Potentially yes |

Exact behaviour differs between exchanges.

Never assume two platforms implement margin identically.

---

# Why Cross Margin Can Surprise Beginners

A beginner may think:

> “I'm only risking the $500 shown on this trade.”

But with cross margin, additional account balance may be supporting the position.

That can result in a much larger amount of capital being consumed
