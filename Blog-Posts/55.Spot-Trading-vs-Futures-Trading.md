# What Is Spot Trading?

Spot trading means buying or selling the actual asset at the current market.

If you buy:

## **1 ETH**

on a spot market, you now own:

## **1 ETH**

on that platform.

You can usually:

- hold it
- sell it later
- withdraw it
- send it to another wallet
- use it on-chain if withdrawals are supported

You are trading the underlying asset itself.

---

# A Simple Spot Trade

Imagine Bitcoin trades at:

## **$100,000**

You buy:

## **0.01 BTC**

Your purchase costs approximately:

## **$1,000**

ignoring fees.

If Bitcoin rises to:

## **$120,000**

your 0.01 BTC would be worth approximately:

## **$1,200**

If Bitcoin falls to:

## **$80,000**

your BTC would be worth approximately:

## **$800**

You still own:

## **0.01 BTC**

The market value simply changed.

---

# There Is Normally No Liquidation in Unleveraged Spot Trading

This is one of the biggest differences.

Suppose you buy Bitcoin at:

## $100,000

and it falls to:

## $50,000

If you simply bought Bitcoin with your own money and used no borrowing or margin, the exchange does not normally liquidate your BTC just because the price fell.

You still hold the asset.

Its value has decreased, but your position is not automatically closed.

That is very different from leveraged futures trading.

---

# What Is Futures Trading?

A futures market allows traders to trade a:

# **Contract**

whose value is linked to an underlying asset.

Instead of necessarily buying Bitcoin itself, you may be trading a contract based on Bitcoin's price.

For example:

You could open a:

## BTC Long

without owning actual BTC.

Or a:

## BTC Short

that benefits if Bitcoin's price falls.

This is one of the main reasons futures are popular with active traders.

---

# Spot Means Asset — Futures Means Contract

The simplest distinction is:

## Spot Trading

You trade the actual asset.

## Futures Trading

You trade a contract based on the asset's price.

This difference affects:

- ownership
- leverage
- shorting
- liquidation
- funding
- margin
- settlement

---

# Example: Spot Bitcoin

Bitcoin price:

## $100,000

You spend:

## $1,000

You receive approximately:

## 0.01 BTC

You now own that BTC balance.

If the price moves, the value of your BTC changes.

---

# Example: Bitcoin Futures

Bitcoin price:

## $100,000

Instead of buying Bitcoin, you open:

## $10,000 BTC long exposure

through a futures contract.

You may only provide:

## $1,000 margin

if using 10× leverage.

You do not necessarily own:

## 0.1 BTC

You own or control a derivatives position whose profit and loss follows Bitcoin's price.

That distinction matters.

---

# Spot Trading Is Naturally Long

In normal spot trading:

1. You buy an asset.
2. You own the asset.
3. You hope its value rises.
4. You sell later.

This naturally creates:

## **Long Exposure**

If the asset rises, you benefit.

If it falls, your holding loses value.

---

# Futures Can Go Long or Short

Futures make it easy to trade either direction.

You can:

## 📈 Go Long

if you believe price will rise.

Or:

## 📉 Go Short

if you believe price will fall.

You don't necessarily need to own the underlying crypto first.

---

# Spot Shorting Is More Complicated

If you simply own Bitcoin and sell it, you are not automatically short.

You just reduced your Bitcoin holdings.

To short in a spot-style system, you may need to:

- borrow the asset
- sell it
- buy it back later
- return the borrowed amount

Futures simplify this process by allowing short exposure through derivatives.

---

# What Are Traditional Futures?

Traditional futures contracts generally have:

## **An Expiry Date**

For example, a Bitcoin futures contract might settle on:

## December 31

At expiry, the contract is settled according to the exchange's rules.

Traditional futures can trade above or below the current spot price depending on:

- interest rates
- market expectations
- demand
- time until expiry
- carrying costs

---

# What Are Perpetual Futures?

Crypto markets commonly use:

# **Perpetual Futures**

often called:

# **Perps**

Perpetual futures are derivative contracts designed to track an underlying asset but usually have:

## **No fixed expiry date**

You can potentially keep the position open as long as:

- you maintain enough margin
- you avoid liquidation
- the market remains available
- you pay or receive applicable funding

---

# Why Do Perpetual Futures Need Funding?

If a perpetual contract never expires, something is needed to help keep its price reasonably close to the underlying spot market.

That mechanism is commonly:

# **Funding**

At scheduled intervals, payments may occur between long and short traders.

Depending on the market:

- longs may pay shorts
- shorts may pay longs

The exact funding system varies by platform.

---

# Positive Funding

In many crypto perpetual markets:

## Positive funding

usually means:

## **Longs pay shorts**

This can happen when bullish demand pushes the perpetual market above the underlying spot price.

---

# Negative Funding

When funding is negative:

## **Shorts may pay longs**

This can happen when bearish positioning dominates.

Funding is not guaranteed to stay positive or negative.

It changes with market conditions.

---

# Why Funding Matters

Suppose your trade is slightly profitable.

But you have held a leveraged perpetual position for a long time and repeatedly paid funding.

Those costs reduce your actual return.

A trade can move in the correct direction and still be less profitable than expected because of:

- funding
- fees
- spread
- slippage

---

# Spot Trading Has No Perpetual Funding Rate

If you simply buy Bitcoin on the spot market and hold it, you do not normally pay a perpetual futures funding rate.

You may still encounter:

- trading fees
- withdrawal fees
- custody costs
- network fees

But the derivatives funding mechanism does not apply to the same ordinary spot holding.

---

# Leverage Is Common in Futures

One of the biggest attractions of futures is:

## **Leverage**

A trader may be able to choose:

- 2×
- 5×
- 10×
- 20×
- 50×
- 100×

depending on the platform and asset.

This allows larger exposure with less initial collateral.

But as we covered in Lesson #54:

## Leverage magnifies losses as well as gains.

---

# Spot Can Also Use Margin

It is important not to oversimplify.

Some exchanges offer:

## **Spot Margin Trading**

where traders can borrow funds or assets while trading spot markets.

So spot does not always mean:

## “No leverage.”

But ordinary unleveraged spot trading is usually much simpler than leveraged futures.

---

# Example: Spot vs Futures Long

Imagine Bitcoin rises:

## $100,000 → $105,000

That's a:

## 5% increase

### Spot Trader

Invests:

## $1,000

Profit:

approximately:

## $50

before fees.

### Futures Trader

Uses:

## $1,000 margin

with:

## 10× leverage

to control $10,000 exposure.

A 5% move could produce approximately:

## $500 profit

before funding, fees and other costs.

That sounds great.

But reverse the movement.

A 5% decline could create approximately:

## $500 loss

for the futures trader.

The spot trader's $1,000 holding would simply fall to around:

## $950

---

# The Futures Trader Has Liquidation Risk

Suppose a futures position becomes too unprofitable.

If the trader no longer has enough margin supporting the position, the exchange can:

# **Liquidate it**

The trader's position is forcibly closed.

This does not normally happen to a fully paid, unleveraged spot holding.

---

# Spot Example During a Crash

You buy:

## $1,000 of Bitcoin

Bitcoin falls:

## 30%

Your holding becomes worth approximately:

## $700

Painful.

But you still own the Bitcoin.

If Bitcoin later recovers, the value can recover with it.

---

# Futures Example During the Same Crash

You use:

## $1,000 margin

to open a highly leveraged Bitcoin long.

Bitcoin falls sharply.

Your position may be liquidated long before Bitcoin reaches:

## -30%

Once liquidation occurs:

## The position is gone.

If Bitcoin later recovers, you no longer benefit unless you open another position.

---

# Being Right Eventually Is Not Enough in Futures

This is one of the biggest differences.

Suppose you believe:

> “Bitcoin will eventually reach $120,000.”

You open a highly leveraged long at:

## $100,000

Bitcoin first falls to:

## $95,000

Your position gets liquidated.

A week later Bitcoin reaches:

## $120,000

Your prediction was eventually correct.

Your trade still lost.

In futures:

## **The path matters.**

---

# Ownership Is Different

This is another important distinction.

If you buy spot ETH:

## You own ETH.

If you buy an ETH futures contract:

## You own a derivatives position.

That means futures generally cannot simply be withdrawn to your wallet as ETH.

You would first need to close or settle the derivatives position and then acquire actual ETH separately.

---

# Why Would Someone Trade Futures Instead of Spot?

There are several reasons.

Futures can offer:

- short exposure
- leverage
- hedging
- capital efficiency
- derivatives strategies
- easier directional trading

For experienced traders, futures can be useful tools.

But they add complexity and risk.

---

# Why Would Someone Prefer Spot?

Spot can be simpler.

You buy an asset.

You own it.

There is usually:

- no liquidation on unleveraged holdings
- no perpetual funding
- no futures margin system
- no contract expiry
- less complexity

For many beginners, understanding spot markets first creates a better foundation before moving into derivatives.

---

# Futures Can Be Used for Hedging

Not everyone uses futures for speculation.

Suppose you own:

## $100,000 worth of Bitcoin

for the long term.

You are worried the market may fall temporarily.

You do not want to sell your Bitcoin.

You could potentially open a smaller:

## Bitcoin short futures position

If Bitcoin falls:

- your spot holdings lose value
- your futures short gains value

This can offset some of the downside.

That is:

## **Hedging**

---

# A Hedge Does Not Eliminate Risk

Hedging can create other issues.

For example:

- funding costs
- fees
- imperfect sizing
- liquidation risk
- timing errors

If Bitcoin rises instead:

- the spot position gains
- the short hedge loses

The hedge changes the exposure.

It does not magically remove all risk.

---

# Futures Price vs Spot Price

The futures price does not always perfectly equal the spot price.

For example:

## Spot BTC

$100,000

## Futures BTC

$100,200

or:

## $99,800

Small differences can occur because futures markets have their own:

- buyers
- sellers
- leverage
- liquidity
- expectations
