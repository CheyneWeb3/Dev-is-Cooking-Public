# **Spot Trading**

and:

# **Futures Trading**

At first glance, they can look very similar.

You might see the same Bitcoin chart.

The same price.

The same Buy and Sell buttons.

But underneath, they are very different products.

So let's cook it down.

# What Is Spot Trading?

Spot trading means buying or selling the actual asset at the current market.

If you buy:

## **1 ETH**

on a spot market, you now own:

## **1 ETH**

on that platform.

You can usually:

- hold it
- sell it later
- withdraw it
- send it to another wallet
- use it on-chain if withdrawals are supported

You are trading the underlying asset itself.

---

# A Simple Spot Trade

Imagine Bitcoin trades at:

## **$100,000**

You buy:

## **0.01 BTC**

Your purchase costs approximately:

## **$1,000**

ignoring fees.

If Bitcoin rises to:

## **$120,000**

your 0.01 BTC would be worth approximately:

## **$1,200**

If Bitcoin falls to:

## **$80,000**

your BTC would be worth approximately:

## **$800**

You still own:

## **0.01 BTC**

The market value simply changed.

---

# There Is Normally No Liquidation in Unleveraged Spot Trading

This is one of the biggest differences.

Suppose you buy Bitcoin at:

## $100,000

and it falls to:

## $50,000

If you simply bought Bitcoin with your own money and used no borrowing or margin, the exchange does not normally liquidate your BTC just because the price fell.

You still hold the asset.

Its value has decreased, but your position is not automatically closed.

That is very different from leveraged futures trading.

---

# What Is Futures Trading?

A futures market allows traders to trade a:

# **Contract**

whose value is linked to an underlying asset.

Instead of necessarily buying Bitcoin itself, you may be trading a contract based on Bitcoin's price.

For example:

You could open a:

## BTC Long

without owning actual BTC.

Or a:

## BTC Short

that benefits if Bitcoin's price falls.

This is one of the main reasons futures are popular with active traders.

---

# Spot Means Asset — Futures Means Contract

The simplest distinction is:

## Spot Trading

You trade the actual asset.

## Futures Trading

You trade a contract based on the asset's price.

This difference affects:

- ownership
- leverage
- shorting
- liquidation
- funding
- margin
- settlement

---

# Example: Spot Bitcoin

Bitcoin price:

## $100,000

You spend:

## $1,000

You receive approximately:

## 0.01 BTC

You now own that BTC balance.

If the price moves, the value of your BTC changes.

---

# Example: Bitcoin Futures

Bitcoin price:

## $100,000

Instead of buying Bitcoin, you open:

## $10,000 BTC long exposure

through a futures contract.

You may only provide:

## $1,000 margin

if using 10× leverage.

You do not necessarily own:

## 0.1 BTC

You own or control a derivatives position whose profit and loss follows Bitcoin's price.

That distinction matters.

---

# Spot Trading Is Naturally Long

In normal spot trading:

1. You buy an asset.
2. You own the asset.
3. You hope its value rises.
4. You sell later.

This naturally creates:

## **Long Exposure**

If the asset rises, you benefit.

If it falls, your holding loses value.

---

# Futures Can Go Long or Short

Futures make it easy to trade either direction.

You can:

## 📈 Go Long

if you believe price will rise.

Or:

## 📉 Go Short

if you believe price will fall.

You don't necessarily need to own the underlying crypto first.

---

# Spot Shorting Is More Complicated

If you simply own Bitcoin and sell it, you are not automatically short.

You just reduced your Bitcoin holdings.

To short in a spot-style system, you may need to:

- borrow the asset
- sell it
- buy it back later
- return the borrowed amount

Futures simplify this process by allowing short exposure through derivatives.

---

# What Are Traditional Futures?

Traditional futures contracts generally have:

## **An Expiry Date**

For example, a Bitcoin futures contract might settle on:

## December 31

At expiry, the contract is settled according to the exchange's rules.

Traditional futures can trade above or below the current spot price depending on:

- interest rates
- market expectations
- demand
- time until expiry
- carrying costs

---

# What Are Perpetual Futures?

Crypto markets commonly use:

# **Perpetual Futures**

often called:

# **Perps**

Perpetual futures are derivative contracts designed to track an underlying asset but usually have:

## **No fixed expiry date**

You can potentially keep the position open as long as:

- you maintain enough margin
- you avoid liquidation
- the market remains available
- you pay or receive applicable funding

---

# Why Do Perpetual Futures Need Funding?

If a perpetual contract never expires, something is needed to help keep its price reasonably close to the underlying spot market.

That mechanism is commonly:

# **Funding**

At scheduled intervals, payments may occur between long and short traders.

Depending on the market:

- longs may pay shorts
- shorts may pay longs

The exact funding system varies by platform.

---

# Positive Funding

In many crypto perpetual markets:

## Positive funding

usually means:

## **Longs pay shorts**

This can happen when bullish demand pushes the perpetual market above the underlying spot price.

---

# Negative Funding

When funding is negative:

## **Shorts may pay longs**

This can happen when bearish positioning dominates.

Funding is not guaranteed to stay positive or negative.

It changes with market conditions.

---

# Why Funding Matters

Suppose your trade is slightly profitable.

But you have held a leveraged perpetual position for a long time and repeatedly paid funding.

Those costs reduce your actual return.

A trade can move in the correct direction and still be less profitable than expected because of:

- funding
- fees
- spread
- slippage

---

# Spot Trading Has No Perpetual Funding Rate

If you simply buy Bitcoin on the spot market and hold it, you do not normally pay a perpetual futures funding rate.

You may still encounter:

- trading fees
- withdrawal fees
- custody costs
- network fees

But the derivatives funding mechanism does not apply to the same ordinary spot holding.

---

# Leverage Is Common in Futures

One of the biggest attractions of futures is:

## **Leverage**

A trader may be able to choose:

- 2×
- 5×
- 10×
- 20×
- 50×
- 100×

depending on the platform and asset.

This allows larger exposure with less initial collateral.

But as we covered in Lesson #54:

## Leverage magnifies losses as well as gains.

---

# Spot Can Also Use Margin

It is important not to oversimplify.

Some exchanges offer:

## **Spot Margin Trading**

where traders can borrow funds or assets while trading spot markets.

So spot does not always mean:

## “No leverage.”

But ordinary unleveraged spot trading is usually much simpler than leveraged futures.

---

# Example: Spot vs Futures Long

Imagine Bitcoin rises:

## $100,000 → $105,000

That's a:

## 5% increase

### Spot Trader

Invests:

## $1,000

Profit:

approximately:

## $50

before fees.

### Futures Trader

Uses:

## $1,000 margin

with:

## 10× leverage

to control $10,000 exposure.

A 5% move could produce approximately:

## $500 profit

before funding, fees and other costs.

That sounds great.

But reverse the movement.

A 5% decline could create approximately:

## $500 loss

for the futures trader.

The spot trader's $1,000 holding would simply fall to around:

## $950

---

# The Futures Trader Has Liquidation Risk

Suppose a futures position becomes too unprofitable.

If the trader no longer has enough margin supporting the position, the exchange can:

# **Liquidate it**

The trader's position is forcibly closed.

This does not normally happen to a fully paid, unleveraged spot holding.

---

# Spot Example During a Crash

You buy:

## $1,000 of Bitcoin

Bitcoin falls:

## 30%

Your holding becomes worth approximately:

## $700

Painful.

But you still own the Bitcoin.

If Bitcoin later recovers, the value can recover with it.

---

# Futures Example During the Same Crash

You use:

## $1,000 margin

to open a highly leveraged Bitcoin long.

Bitcoin falls sharply.

Your position may be liquidated long before Bitcoin reaches:

## -30%

Once liquidation occurs:

## The position is gone.

If Bitcoin later recovers, you no longer benefit unless you open another position.

---

# Being Right Eventually Is Not Enough in Futures

This is one of the biggest differences.

Suppose you believe:

> “Bitcoin will eventually reach $120,000.”

You open a highly leveraged long at:

## $100,000

Bitcoin first falls to:

## $95,000

Your position gets liquidated.

A week later Bitcoin reaches:

## $120,000

Your prediction was eventually correct.

Your trade still lost.

In futures:

## **The path matters.**

---

# Ownership Is Different

This is another important distinction.

If you buy spot ETH:

## You own ETH.

If you buy an ETH futures contract:

## You own a derivatives position.

That means futures generally cannot simply be withdrawn to your wallet as ETH.

You would first need to close or settle the derivatives position and then acquire actual ETH separately.

---

# Why Would Someone Trade Futures Instead of Spot?

There are several reasons.

Futures can offer:

- short exposure
- leverage
- hedging
- capital efficiency
- derivatives strategies
- easier directional trading

For experienced traders, futures can be useful tools.

But they add complexity and risk.

---

# Why Would Someone Prefer Spot?

Spot can be simpler.

You buy an asset.

You own it.

There is usually:

- no liquidation on unleveraged holdings
- no perpetual funding
- no futures margin system
- no contract expiry
- less complexity

For many beginners, understanding spot markets first creates a better foundation before moving into derivatives.

---

# Futures Can Be Used for Hedging

Not everyone uses futures for speculation.

Suppose you own:

## $100,000 worth of Bitcoin

for the long term.

You are worried the market may fall temporarily.

You do not want to sell your Bitcoin.

You could potentially open a smaller:

## Bitcoin short futures position

If Bitcoin falls:

- your spot holdings lose value
- your futures short gains value

This can offset some of the downside.

That is:

## **Hedging**

---

# A Hedge Does Not Eliminate Risk

Hedging can create other issues.

For example:

- funding costs
- fees
- imperfect sizing
- liquidation risk
- timing errors

If Bitcoin rises instead:

- the spot position gains
- the short hedge loses

The hedge changes the exposure.

It does not magically remove all risk.

---

# Futures Price vs Spot Price

The futures price does not always perfectly equal the spot price.

For example:

## Spot BTC

$100,000

## Futures BTC

$100,200

or:

## $99,800

Small differences can occur because futures markets have their own:

- buyers
- sellers
- leverage
- liquidity
- expectations

These differences can also create arbitrage opportunities.

---

# What Is Basis?

The difference between the futures price and spot price is often called:

# **Basis**

For example:

Spot:

## $100,000

Futures:

## $102,000

Basis:

## $2,000

or approximately:

## 2%

Traditional futures basis can change depending on how much time remains until expiry.

---

# Contango

When futures trade above the spot price, the market may be described as being in:

# **Contango**

For example:

Spot BTC:

## $100,000

Futures:

## $103,000

This can reflect various market and carrying-cost factors.

---

# Backwardation

When futures trade below spot, the market may be in:

# **Backwardation**

For example:

Spot BTC:

## $100,000

Futures:

## $98,000

These concepts become more useful as you move into advanced derivatives trading.

---

# Futures Use Margin

To open a futures position, you normally provide:

# **Margin**

Margin is collateral supporting the position.

For example:

## Position size

$10,000

## Margin

$2,000

Your position has:

## 5× leverage

in this simplified example.

---

# Initial Margin vs Maintenance Margin

Two important terms are:

## Initial Margin

The collateral required to open the position.

## Maintenance Margin

The minimum collateral required to keep the position open.

If your account falls below the required maintenance level:

## Liquidation can begin.

---

# Isolated Futures Margin

With:

## **Isolated Margin**

you assign collateral specifically to one position.

For example:

## $500

might support one BTC position.

The risk is more contained to that position according to the platform's rules.

---

# Cross Futures Margin

With:

## **Cross Margin**

more of your account's available balance may support open positions.

This can prevent liquidation for longer.

But it can expose more of the account to losses.

Always understand which margin mode you're using.

---

# Spot Traders Can Hold Through Volatility

Imagine ETH falls:

## 40%

If you own ETH spot with no leverage, you can choose to continue holding.

Whether that is a good decision is another question.

But the market normally does not force you out.

---

# Futures Traders May Not Have That Choice

A highly leveraged futures trader may be liquidated after a much smaller movement.

The exchange decides the position cannot remain open.

So:

## Spot gives more tolerance for price movement.

## Futures can dramatically reduce that tolerance.

---

# Shorting Is One of the Biggest Futures Advantages

Suppose you believe Bitcoin will fall.

In spot trading, you could:

## Sell Bitcoin you already own.

But once you own zero BTC, you cannot simply keep selling BTC you don't own unless borrowing is available.

Futures allow you to open:

## Short exposure

without first owning Bitcoin.

This makes bearish trading much easier.

---

# Futures Traders Can Profit in Falling Markets

Example:

Bitcoin:

## $100,000

You short.

Bitcoin falls to:

## $90,000

Your short position gains value.

A spot holder would generally lose value during the same price decline.

This makes futures useful for traders who want to trade both market directions.

---

# But Shorting Adds Different Risks

Remember from Lesson #53:

A long asset can theoretically fall to:

## $0

A short faces an asset that theoretically has:

## No fixed maximum price.

With leverage added, a rapid upward move can liquidate a short extremely quickly.

---

# Spot Trading Still Has Risk

Spot is simpler.

That does not mean:

## Safe.

A spot token can:

- fall 50%
- fall 90%
- go to zero
- lose liquidity
- be exploited
- be abandoned
- become impossible to sell

There may be no liquidation engine, but there is still market risk.

---

# Futures Add Extra Layers of Risk

Futures can include:

- leverage risk
- liquidation risk
- funding risk
- margin risk
- counterparty risk
- contract risk
- basis risk
- execution risk

This is on top of normal market risk.

---

# Example: Same Token, Different Risks

Imagine TOKEN X is:

## $100

### Spot

You buy one token for $100.

Worst theoretical price outcome:

## TOKEN X → $0

Your $100 investment becomes worthless.

### Leveraged Futures

You use $100 margin to control:

## $1,000 of TOKEN X exposure.

The token does not need to go anywhere near zero to destroy your margin.

A relatively small adverse movement could liquidate you.

---

# Spot Trading and Self-Custody

Another important difference:

If you buy actual crypto on spot, you may be able to withdraw it into:

## Your own wallet.

This allows:

# **Self-Custody**

You control the private keys.

With a futures contract, there is usually no underlying token to withdraw.

The position exists inside the trading system or protocol.

---

# Futures and Counterparty Risk

On a centralised futures exchange, you depend on the platform to:

- custody collateral
- calculate positions
- maintain the matching engine
- process liquidations
- honour withdrawals

This creates exchange or counterparty risk.

Spot assets held on a centralised exchange also face custody risk until withdrawn.

---

# Decentralised Futures

Crypto also has:

# **Decentralised Perpetual Exchanges**

These allow users to trade leveraged long and short positions through blockchain-based protocols.

They may use:

- smart contracts
- liquidity pools
- order books
- collateral vaults
- price oracles
- keeper networks
- liquidation bots

The mechanics vary considerably between protocols.

---

# DEX Futures Still Have Risk

“Decentralised” does not mean:

## Risk-free.

Possible risks include:

- smart contract bugs
- oracle failures
- insufficient liquidity
- network congestion
- liquidation problems
- protocol insolvency
- governance risk

Always understand the system you are using.

---

# Order Types Exist in Both Markets

Spot and futures markets can both support orders such as:

- market orders
- limit orders
- stop losses
- take-profit orders

But futures platforms may add additional settings such as:

- reduce-only
- post-only
- trigger price
- mark price
- index price
- margin mode
- leverage

These settings matter.

---

# What Is Reduce-Only?

A:

## **Reduce-Only Order**

is designed to only reduce or close an existing derivatives position.

It should not increase the position or accidentally create one in the opposite direction.

For example:

You are long:

## 1 BTC

You place a reduce-only sell for:

## 1 BTC.

Its purpose is to close or reduce that long.

Not open a new short.

This can be useful for stop-loss and take-profit orders.

---

# Why Reduce-Only Matters

Imagine you are long:

## 1 BTC

You place a take-profit sell for:

## 1 BTC

But you manually close the long first.

If the old sell order remains active and is not reduce-only, it could potentially open a:

## Short position

when triggered.

Reduce-only can help prevent that, depending on the platform.

---

# Mark Price

Many futures exchanges use a:

# **Mark Price**

for calculations such as liquidation.

The mark price may be designed to represent a fairer market value than the most recent trade.

This helps reduce the effect of temporary price manipulation or unusual trades.

---

# Last Price

The:

## **Last Price**

is simply the price of the most recent trade.

It can sometimes differ from:

## Mark Price

and:

## Index Price.

Understanding which price your exchange uses for liquidation and triggers is extremely important.

---

# Index Price

An:

## **Index Price**

may combine spot prices from multiple exchanges or data sources.

It can be used as a reference for:

- mark prices
- funding
- derivatives pricing

Again, platforms vary.

---

# A Futures Chart Can Show One Price While Liquidation Uses Another

This surprises beginners.

You might see:

## Last Price: $100,000

while the:

## Mark Price: $99,800

Your liquidation calculations may use the mark price rather than the last trade.

Always know which price matters for your position.

---

# Spot Trading Is Often Better for Learning the Basics

Before using futures, it helps to understand:

- market orders
- limit orders
- stop losses
- take profits
- liquidity
- slippage
- volatility
- position sizing

Futures add:

- leverage
- margin
- liquidation
- funding
- derivatives pricing

Learning everything at once can be expensive.

---

# Futures Are Not “Advanced Spot”

This is worth remembering.

Futures are not simply:

## “Spot trading but better.”

They are a different financial instrument.

They can provide capabilities spot does not.

But those capabilities come with additional complexity.

---

# Why Traders Get Into Trouble

A beginner often thinks:

> “I only have $100, so futures lets me trade like I have $1,000.”

Technically, leverage may allow that exposure.

But it also means:

## They are taking $1,000-sized market risk with only $100 supporting it.

A small move can therefore become devastating.

---

# The Spot Trader's Question

A spot trader usually asks:

> **“Do I think this asset will rise enough to justify buying it?”**

---

# The Futures Trader Has More Questions

A futures trader needs to ask:

- Long or short?
- How much leverage?
- What margin mode?
- Where is liquidation?
- What's the funding rate?
- What is the mark price?
- How large is the position?
- Where is the stop?
- How much can I lose?

More flexibility means more decisions.

---

# Spot vs Futures Comparison

| Feature | Spot | Futures |
|---|---|---|
| Own underlying asset | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Long exposure | Yes | Yes |
| Short exposure | Requires extra mechanism | Common |
| Leverage | Optional / margin-specific | Common |
| Liquidation | Not on normal unleveraged spot | Yes when leveraged |
| Funding rates | No perpetual funding | Common on perpetuals |
| Expiry | No | Some futures expire |
| Perpetual contracts | No | Yes |
| Self-custody possible | Usually yes | Contract itself generally cannot be withdrawn |
| Complexity | Lower | Higher |

# When Might Someone Choose Spot?

Spot may suit someone who wants to:

- buy actual crypto
- hold an asset
- withdraw to self-custody
- avoid liquidation
- avoid perpetual funding
- keep trading mechanics simpler

---

# When Might Someone Use Futures?

Futures may be used for:

- shorting
- hedging
- leverage
- capital efficiency
- derivatives strategies
- active trading

But these benefits come with additional risk.

---

# Common Beginner Spot Mistakes

## 1. Buying Without Research

Owning the actual token does not make a bad project safer.

## 2. Ignoring Liquidity

A token may be difficult to sell.

## 3. Chasing Pumps

Buying after large price moves because of FOMO.

## 4. Holding Forever With No Plan

“No liquidation” does not mean losses should be ignored indefinitely.

---

# Common Beginner Futures Mistakes

## 1. Using Too Much Leverage

Normal volatility becomes dangerous.

## 2. Ignoring Liquidation

The trader doesn't know how close forced closure is.

## 3. Confusing Margin With Position Size

They underestimate actual exposure.

## 4. Ignoring Funding

Holding costs accumulate.

## 5. No Stop Loss

The position runs directly toward liquidation.

## 6. Treating Futures Like Gambling

Random high-leverage bets are not a trading strategy.

---

# Before Trading Spot

Know:

- what asset you're buying
- the market price
- liquidity
- fees
- your position size
- your exit plan
- where the asset will be stored

---

# Before Trading Futures

Know all of the above plus:

- contract type
- leverage
- margin
- isolated or cross
- liquidation price
- funding rate
- mark price
- trigger price
- total notional exposure
- maximum acceptable loss

If you cannot explain those terms, learning them before opening a leveraged futures trade is worth the effort.

---

# The Key Lesson

Spot and futures can display the same crypto asset while representing completely different types of trade.

Remember:

### 🪙 SPOT

**You generally buy and own the underlying asset.**

### 📄 FUTURES

**You trade a contract based on the asset's price.**

### ⚙️ LEVERAGE

**Common in futures and magnifies exposure.**

### ⚠️ LIQUIDATION

**A leveraged futures position can be forcibly closed.**

### 💸 FUNDING

**Perpetual futures may involve recurring payments between longs and shorts.**

Spot trading can still produce major losses.

Futures trading adds an entirely new set of risks on top.

Before deciding which market to use, understand what you actually own, what can force you out of the trade, and exactly how much capital is at risk.
