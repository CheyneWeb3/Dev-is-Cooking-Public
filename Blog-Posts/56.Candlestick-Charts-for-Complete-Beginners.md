# What Is a Candlestick?

A candlestick represents the price movement of an asset during a specific period of time.

That period might be:

- 1 minute
- 5 minutes
- 15 minutes
- 1 hour
- 4 hours
- 1 day
- 1 week

or another timeframe selected on the chart.

Every candle tells you four important prices:

# **O — Open**

# **H — High**

# **L — Low**

# **C — Close**

Together these are often called:

# **OHLC**

---

# The Four Ingredients of Every Candle

Let's look at them individually.

## Open

The:

# **Open**

is the price at the beginning of that candle's time period.

If you're looking at a one-hour candle beginning at 2:00 PM, the open is approximately the price when that candle began at 2:00 PM.

---

## High

The:

# **High**

is the highest price reached during that candle.

Even if the price only touched that level briefly, it becomes part of the candle's recorded range.

---

## Low

The:

# **Low**

is the lowest price reached during that period.

Again, the market does not need to remain there.

It simply has to trade at that level during the candle.

---

## Close

The:

# **Close**

is the final price when that candle's time period ends.

For many traders, the close is particularly important because it shows where the market finished the period.

---

# A Simple Candle Example

Imagine we're looking at a:

## **1-Hour BTC Candle**

During that hour:

**Open:** $100,000  
**High:** $103,000  
**Low:** $99,000  
**Close:** $102,000

That one candle tells us:

Bitcoin started the hour around:

## $100,000

At some point it traded as high as:

## $103,000

At some point it traded as low as:

## $99,000

And when the hour ended, it was around:

## $102,000

Four pieces of information.

One candle.

---

# What Makes a Candle Green?

A candle is commonly displayed as:

## 🟢 Green

when the closing price is higher than the opening price.

For example:

**Open:** $100

**Close:** $110

Price finished the period higher than where it started.

That produces a bullish candle on most chart settings.

---

# What Makes a Candle Red?

A candle is commonly displayed as:

## 🔴 Red

when the closing price is lower than the opening price.

For example:

**Open:** $100

**Close:** $90

The market finished the period lower than where it began.

That produces a bearish candle on most chart settings.

---

# Green Usually Means Price Closed Higher

A beginner might think:

> “Green means everyone made money.”

Not necessarily.

It simply means:

## **The candle closed above its opening price.**

Someone who bought near the candle's high could still be losing money.

Someone who shorted near the low could also be losing money.

The candle colour only describes the relationship between:

## Open

and:

## Close.

---

# Red Doesn't Mean the Asset Is Crashing

Likewise, one red candle does not automatically mean:

> “The market is collapsing.”

It simply means:

## The candle closed lower than it opened.

A red one-minute candle might represent a tiny movement inside a much larger multi-month uptrend.

Context matters.

---

# The Candle Body

The thick rectangular section of the candle is called the:

# **Body**

The body represents the distance between:

## Open

and:

## Close.

For a green candle:

the bottom of the body commonly represents the open and the top represents the close.

For a red candle:

the top commonly represents the open and the bottom represents the close.

---

# Example of a Large Green Body

Imagine:

**Open:** $100

**Close:** $120

That creates a relatively large green body.

This tells us buyers pushed the market substantially higher during that period.

But it still doesn't tell us everything.

We also need to look at the:

# **Wicks**

---

# What Are Candle Wicks?

The thin lines extending above and below the candle body are called:

# **Wicks**

You may also hear them called:

# **Shadows**

The upper wick shows how far price traded above the candle body.

The lower wick shows how far price traded below the body.

Together, they reveal prices the market visited but did not maintain by the candle close.

---

# Upper Wick

Suppose a candle:

**Opens:** $100

**Trades to:** $120

**Closes:** $105

The market reached $120.

But it could not remain there.

The candle therefore has a large:

## **Upper Wick**

That tells us there was trading activity significantly above the eventual closing price.

---

# Lower Wick

Now imagine:

**Open:** $100

**Trades down to:** $80

**Closes:** $98

Price fell heavily during the period but recovered before the candle closed.

That leaves a long:

## **Lower Wick**

It shows that the market visited much lower prices before buyers pushed it back upward.

---

# Wicks Show Rejected or Temporary Price Areas

You will often hear traders describe a wick as:

## **Price rejection**

For example, a long upper wick may suggest the market attempted to move higher but encountered selling.

A long lower wick may suggest price moved lower and encountered buying.

But be careful.

A wick does not automatically mean the market must reverse.

It simply shows:

## **Where price traded during that period.**

Interpretation requires context.

---

# A Complete Green Candle Example

Imagine:

**Open:** $100  
**High:** $120  
**Low:** $95  
**Close:** $115

The candle tells us:

Price opened at:

## $100

fell as low as:

## $95

then climbed as high as:

## $120

before closing at:

## $115

Because $115 is above $100:

## The candle is green.

The candle body runs approximately:

## $100 → $115

The lower wick runs:

## $100 → $95

The upper wick runs:

## $115 → $120

---

# A Complete Red Candle Example

Now imagine:

**Open:** $100  
**High:** $105  
**Low:** $80  
**Close:** $85

Price started at:

## $100

reached:

## $105

fell as low as:

## $80

then finished at:

## $85

Because the close is below the open:

## The candle is red.

---

# Candle Size Tells You Something About Movement

A candle with a large body shows a larger difference between its open and close.

A candle with a tiny body shows that the open and close were relatively close together.

Compare:

### Candle A

Open:

## $100

Close:

## $101

### Candle B

Open:

## $100

Close:

## $120

Candle B experienced a much larger open-to-close movement.

But again:

## Larger does not automatically mean more important.

The timeframe and normal volatility of the asset matter.

---

# What Is a Doji?

A:

# **Doji**

is a candle where the opening and closing prices are very close together.

For example:

**Open:** $100

**Close:** $100.05

The candle body becomes extremely small.

The market may have moved significantly during the period, but it ended close to where it started.

Traders sometimes interpret this as:

## **Indecision**

between buyers and sellers.

But one doji alone does not predict what happens next.

---

# Long Wicks With Small Bodies

Imagine price:

- opens at $100
- rises to $120
- falls to $80
- closes at $101

That candle would have:

- a small body
- a long upper wick
- a long lower wick

This tells us:

## The market moved dramatically in both directions but finished close to where it started.

That can indicate significant volatility and disagreement between buyers and sellers.

---

# Candles Represent Time

This is one of the most important ideas for beginners.

A candlestick does not have one universal meaning.

It depends on the:

# **Timeframe**

A five-minute candle represents:

## 5 minutes of trading.

A one-hour candle represents:

## 1 hour.

A daily candle represents:

## 24 hours.

A weekly candle represents:

## an entire week.

---

# The Same Market Can Look Completely Different on Different Timeframes

Imagine Bitcoin falls during the last five minutes.

On the:

## 5-Minute Chart

you might see a large red candle.

You switch to the:

## Daily Chart

and that same movement might appear as a tiny part of a much larger green candle.

Nothing changed about what happened.

You changed:

# **Perspective**

---

# Example

Suppose Bitcoin starts the day at:

## $100,000

rises to:

## $108,000

then falls during the final five minutes from:

## $108,000 → $106,000

The five-minute chart might show a noticeable red candle.

But the daily candle is still:

## $100,000 → $106,000

which is green.

So someone could say:

> “Bitcoin is dumping!”

while looking at five minutes.

Another person could say:

> “Bitcoin is having a strong green day.”

Both statements could describe different timeframes of the same market.

---

# This Is Why Timeframe Matters

Whenever someone posts a chart, one of the first questions should be:

# **What timeframe is this?**

Without that information, the chart can be misleading.

A dramatic movement on:

## 1 minute

may be almost meaningless on:

## 1 week.

---

# Common Trading Timeframes

Some commonly used timeframes include:

### 1 Minute

Very short-term movement.

Often noisy and extremely fast.

### 5 Minutes

Short-term trading activity.

### 15 Minutes

Used by many short-term traders.

### 1 Hour

Provides more context than very short charts.

### 4 Hours

Popular for viewing broader intraday and multi-day structure.

### 1 Day

Each candle represents one full trading day.

### 1 Week

Useful for much longer-term market structure.

---

# There Is No “Best” Timeframe

The appropriate timeframe depends on what you're trying to do.

A trader holding a position for:

## 10 minutes

may care about very short-term candles.

An investor planning to hold for:

## 5 years

probably does not need to panic over every one-minute candle.

Your timeframe should match:

## Your strategy.

---

# Candles Are Built From Trades

A candlestick isn't magical.

It is simply a visual summary of actual trades that occurred in the market during that period.

If people traded an asset at:

- $100
- $101
- $103
- $99
- $102

the chart records that activity and turns it into a visual candle.

---

# What Happens While a Candle Is Still Open?

This catches beginners out.

Suppose you're watching a:

## 1-Hour Candle

and only:

## 20 minutes

have passed.

That candle is:

# **Not finished yet.**

Its:

- high can change
- low can change
- close can change
- colour can change

A candle that looks strongly green halfway through its period could finish completely red.

---

# Example of a Candle Changing Colour

At the start of the hour:

## Open = $100

Twenty minutes later:

## Price = $110

The candle is green.

Forty minutes later:

## Price = $95

Now the candle is red.

At the end of the hour:

## Price = $105

The final candle closes green.

The appearance changed several times before the candle closed.

---

# Closed Candles vs Open Candles

This is why traders often distinguish between:

## **Open Candles**

and:

## **Closed Candles**

A closed candle has completed its timeframe.

Its OHLC values are final.

An open candle is still developing.

Its final appearance is unknown.

---

# Why Traders Wait for Candle Closes

Imagine resistance is around:

## $100

During an hourly candle, price briefly reaches:

## $103

A beginner might immediately think:

> “Breakout!”

But by the end of the hour, price falls back to:

## $98

The candle only wicked above resistance.

It did not close above it.

Some traders therefore wait for:

## **Candle confirmation**

before treating a price movement as a breakout.

---

# What Is a Bullish Candle?

A:

## **Bullish Candle**

usually means:

## Close > Open

The market finished the period higher than where it started.

It is commonly displayed green.

---

# What Is a Bearish Candle?

A:

## **Bearish Candle**

usually means:

## Close < Open

The market finished the period lower than where it started.

It is commonly displayed red.

---

# Bullish Does Not Mean Guaranteed Up Next

This is extremely important.

A huge green candle does not mean:

> “The next candle will definitely be green.”

Likewise, a huge red candle does not guarantee another decline.

Candles show:

## **What happened**

not:

## **What must happen next.**

---

# Candlestick Patterns

Once you start learning charts, you'll encounter names such as:

- Doji
- Hammer
- Shooting Star
- Engulfing Candle
- Morning Star
- Evening Star

These patterns attempt to interpret combinations of:

- candle bodies
- wicks
- positioning
- previous price action

They can be useful as descriptive tools.

But they are not magical signals.

---

# The Hammer

A typical:

## **Hammer**

has:

- a relatively small body
- a long lower wick
- little or less upper wick

It shows that price moved significantly lower during the period before recovering.

Some traders see this as potentially bullish when it appears after a decline.

But context matters.

A hammer-shaped candle appearing randomly does not guarantee a reversal.

---

# Shooting Star

A:

## **Shooting Star**

typically has:

- a small body
- a long upper wick
- relatively little lower wick

It shows price moved significantly higher but failed to maintain those levels.

Some traders consider this potentially bearish when it appears after an upward move.

Again:

## Shape alone is not enough.

---

# Bullish Engulfing Candle

A:

## **Bullish Engulfing Pattern**

generally involves a bullish candle whose body is larger than and surrounds the previous bearish candle's body.

Some traders interpret it as a possible shift toward buying pressure.

But it should be considered alongside:

- trend
- support
- volume
- liquidity
- market structure

---

# Bearish Engulfing Candle

The opposite is a:

## **Bearish Engulfing Pattern**

where a larger bearish body surrounds the previous bullish candle's body.

Some traders see it as potential evidence of stronger selling pressure.

Still:

# **No candle pattern guarantees a reversal.**

---

# Candlestick Patterns Are Context, Not Prophecy

This is where beginners often go wrong.

They learn:

> “Hammer = buy.”

> “Shooting star = sell.”

Markets are not that simple.

If one candle pattern could predict markets reliably by itself, trading would be easy.

Instead, candles should be treated as:

## **Information**

that helps describe buyer and seller behaviour.

---

# What Is Volume?

Candlestick charts are frequently displayed alongside:

# **Trading Volume**

Volume shows how much of the asset traded during a particular period.

Imagine two identical green candles.

### Candle A

Very low volume.

### Candle B

Extremely high volume.

They look similar in price movement but occurred under very different levels of market participation.

We'll cover volume properly later in the series.

---

# Why Volume Can Add Context

Suppose price breaks above an important level.

If there is:

## Strong trading volume

the move may show significant participation.

If almost nobody is trading, the movement may be less convincing.

This isn't a universal rule.

But it demonstrates why candles should rarely be interpreted alone.

---

# Candles and Liquidity

A candle can also look dramatic because a market has very little liquidity.

Imagine a tiny token where one buyer causes price to jump:

## 20%

The chart produces a huge green candle.

But perhaps only:

## $500

was traded.

Compare that with Bitcoin moving 5% while billions of dollars change hands.

The visual candle alone does not tell you how much liquidity supported the move.

---

# This Matters Enormously With Small Crypto Tokens

A low-liquidity chart can produce:

- huge candles
- extreme wicks
- sudden spikes
- sudden crashes

from relatively small trades.

Always ask:

## **How much liquidity is actually behind this chart?**

A dramatic percentage move does not necessarily mean a huge amount of money entered or left the asset.

---

# What Is a Gap?

In some markets, one candle may open at a significantly different price from where the previous candle closed.

This creates a:

# **Gap**

Gaps are common in markets with fixed trading hours because new information can arrive while the exchange is closed.

Crypto trades:

## 24 hours a day

## 7 days a week

so classic session gaps are less common in major continuously traded crypto markets.

However, liquidity gaps and rapid price jumps can still occur.

---

# Candle Charts on Centralised Exchanges

A centralised exchange's candle chart is generally built from trades occurring on:

## That exchange's market.

For example:

BTC/USDT on Exchange A

may have slightly different candles from:

BTC/USDT on Exchange B.

Why?

Because they are separate markets with different buyers and sellers.

---

# DEX Candles Can Differ Too

Decentralised exchange charts may use trades from:

## A particular liquidity pool.

For example:

TOKEN/USDC Pool A

could show a slightly different trading history from:

TOKEN/USDC Pool B.

This matters especially when liquidity is fragmented across multiple DEXs.

---

# There Is Not Always One Universal Crypto Price

Suppose TOKEN X trades at:

## $1.00

on one exchange and:

## $1.03

on another.

Each venue can produce its own candle data.

Arbitrage traders may eventually push those prices closer together.

But at any specific moment, different markets can show slightly different values.

---

# Candlestick Charts Don't Show Everything

Candles are useful.

But they don't directly tell you:

- who bought
- who sold
- why they traded
- total liquidity
- wallet distribution
- token contract risk
- future market direction
- whether a project is legitimate

A beautiful chart does not make a token safe.

---

# Beware of Chart-Only Thinking

Crypto traders sometimes spend hours examining:

- patterns
- indicators
- trend lines

while ignoring:

- liquidity
- contract permissions
- token supply
- unlocks
- market structure
- security
- broader news

Charts are one source of information.

Not the entire market.

---

# How to Read a Candle Step by Step

When looking at any candle, ask:

## 1. What timeframe am I looking at?

A five-minute candle means something very different from a weekly candle.

## 2. Where did it open?

This gives you the starting point.

## 3. Where did it close?

This tells you whether the candle finished higher or lower.

## 4. What was the high?

How far did buyers push price upward?

## 5. What was the low?

How far did sellers push price downward?

## 6. How large is the body?

How significant was the open-to-close movement?

## 7. How large are the wicks?

What prices were visited but not maintained?

## 8. What happened before this candle?

One candle without context tells you very little.

---

# Reading a Sequence of Candles

Candles become much more useful when viewed together.

Imagine:

**Candle 1:** $100 → $105

**Candle 2:** $105 → $110

**Candle 3:** $110 → $115

**Candle 4:** $115 → $120

Rather than one isolated green candle, you're now seeing:

## A sequence of higher prices.

This begins to reveal:

# **Market Structure**

We'll explore that later in the series.

---

# Higher Highs and Higher Lows

When price repeatedly creates:

## Higher highs

and:

## Higher lows

traders commonly describe the market as being in an:

# **Uptrend**

Candles help us visually identify that structure.

---

# Lower Highs and Lower Lows

When price repeatedly produces:

## Lower highs

and:

## Lower lows

the market may be considered in a:

# **Downtrend**

Again, individual candles matter less than the broader structure they create together.

---

# Sideways Markets

Sometimes candles move inside a range.

For example:

## $95 → $105

again and again.

Neither buyers nor sellers manage to create a sustained trend.

This is commonly called:

- ranging
- consolidation
- sideways trading

Not every chart is trending.

---

# Candle Colour Can Be Customised

One small detail:

Green and red are common.

But traders can change chart colours.

Someone might use:

- blue and white
- black and yellow
- purple and orange

So don't rely exclusively on colour.

Understand the relationship between:

## Open and Close.

---

# Logarithmic vs Regular Charts

As you progress, you may encounter:

## Linear Scale

and:

## Logarithmic Scale.

A linear chart treats equal dollar movements equally.

A logarithmic chart treats equal percentage movements more consistently.

For example:

$1 → $2

is:

## +100%

while:

$100 → $101

is only:

## +1%

Long-term crypto charts can look very different depending on the scale.

This is more advanced, but worth knowing exists.

---

# Heikin-Ashi Is Not the Same as Normal Candles

Some platforms offer:

# **Heikin-Ashi Candles**

These use calculations based on current and previous prices to smooth chart movement.

They are not identical to ordinary OHLC candles.

If you're learning candlesticks for the first time:

## Start with standard candlesticks.

Otherwise, you may misunderstand the actual traded open and close prices.

---

# Candles Don't Predict the Future

This deserves its own section.

A chart is historical information.

Even the candle currently forming represents:

## What the market has done up to this moment.

No candle knows:

- tomorrow's news
- the next whale order
- the next liquidation cascade
- the next exploit
- the next regulatory announcement
- the next market panic

Candlestick analysis deals in:

## probabilities and context

not certainty.

# Common Beginner Candlestick Mistakes

## 1. Ignoring the Timeframe

Panicking over a one-minute red candle inside a strong weekly trend.

---

## 2. Trading Before the Candle Closes

A candle can completely change appearance before its timeframe ends.

---

## 3. Treating Green as “Buy”

A green candle simply means close above open.

---

## 4. Treating Red as “Sell”

A red candle simply means close below open.

---

## 5. Memorising Patterns Without Context

A hammer doesn't automatically mean the market will rise.

---

## 6. Ignoring Liquidity

A huge candle can be created by a small trade in an illiquid market.

---

## 7. Ignoring Volume

Price movement without understanding participation gives an incomplete picture.

---

## 8. Looking at Only One Candle

Market structure is built from sequences of price action.

---

## 9. Assuming Every Exchange Has the Exact Same Candle

Different venues can have different prices and liquidity.

# A Simple Beginner Chart Routine

When opening a chart, try this:

### Step 1

Check the asset and trading pair.

Are you looking at:

## BTC/USDC?

## ETH/USDT?

## TOKEN/WRAPPED NATIVE?

The pair matters.

### Step 2

Check the timeframe.

### Step 3

Zoom out.

Don't immediately analyse the last three candles.

### Step 4

Identify the general direction.

Is price:

- rising?
- falling?
- sideways?

### Step 5

Look at highs and lows.

### Step 6

Look at volume and liquidity.

### Step 7

Then inspect the individual candles.

Context first.

Candles second.

# The Key Lesson

A candlestick is simply a visual summary of price movement during a particular period.

Every standard candle contains four main values:

### 🟢 OPEN

**Where the period started.**

### ⬆️ HIGH

**The highest traded price during the period.**

### ⬇️ LOW

**The lowest traded price during the period.**

### 🔴 CLOSE

**Where the period finished.**

The body shows the relationship between:

## Open and Close.

The wicks show:

## High and Low.

And the timeframe tells you:

## How much time that candle represents.

Most importantly:

# **One candle never tells the whole story.**

Charts become useful when you combine candles with:

- timeframe
- market structure
- volume
- liquidity
- risk management
- broader context

Learn what the candle is telling you before trying to predict what comes next.
