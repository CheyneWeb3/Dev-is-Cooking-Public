# **Support and Resistance**

Those levels are often drawn horizontally across a chart.

But markets don't always move sideways.

Sometimes price forms a clear rising structure.

Sometimes it forms a falling structure.

Instead of drawing a horizontal line, traders may use a diagonal line to help visualise the direction of that movement.

That is called a:

# **Trend Line**

Trend lines are simple.

But they are also very easy to misuse.

So let's cook it down.

---

# What Is a Trend Line?

A:

## **Trend Line**

is a line drawn across important highs or lows on a chart to help show the direction and structure of price movement.

A trend line can help traders visualise whether a market is generally:

## 📈 Rising

## 📉 Falling

or moving within a:

## Channel

The line itself does not control price.

It is simply a tool for organising what the chart is already showing.

---

# Rising Trend Line

In an uptrend, traders commonly draw a trend line underneath price by connecting:

# **Higher Lows**

Imagine price moves like this:

## $50 → $70

then falls to:

## $60

then rises to:

## $80

then falls to:

## $68

then rises again.

The important lows are:

## $50

## $60

## $68

Each low is higher than the previous one.

A trader could draw an upward-sloping trend line beneath those lows.

---

# Why Connect the Lows?

In an uptrend, buyers may repeatedly appear at progressively higher prices.

The trend line helps visualise that behaviour.

Instead of support existing at one horizontal level, the potential support area is:

## Rising over time.

This is sometimes called:

# **Dynamic Support**

---

# Falling Trend Line

In a downtrend, traders commonly draw a line above price by connecting:

# **Lower Highs**

Imagine price moves:

## $100 → $70

then rallies to:

## $90

falls to:

## $60

then rallies to:

## $80

The important highs are:

## $100

## $90

## $80

Each high is lower than the previous one.

A downward-sloping line can be drawn across those highs.

---

# Why Connect the Highs?

In a downtrend, sellers may repeatedly appear at progressively lower prices.

The line helps show that the market is struggling to recover to its previous highs.

This can act as:

# **Dynamic Resistance**

---

# Trend Lines Visualise Market Structure

Remember what we learned earlier:

## Uptrend

often contains:

- higher highs
- higher lows

## Downtrend

often contains:

- lower highs
- lower lows

Trend lines help make those structures easier to see.

They do not create the trend.

The trend already exists in the price movement.

---

# A Trend Line Is Not a Prediction

Drawing a line upward does not mean:

> “Price must continue following this line.”

Likewise, a downward trend line does not guarantee:

> “Price will keep falling.”

A trend line describes:

## What price has been doing.

It does not force:

## What price must do next.

---

# Start With Swing Points

Trend lines are usually drawn using:

## Swing Highs

and:

## Swing Lows

A swing high is a local peak.

A swing low is a local bottom.

For example:

## $50 → $70 → $60

The:

## $70

area is a swing high.

The:

## $60

area becomes a swing low if price begins rising again.

---

# Two Points Can Create a Line

Mathematically, two points are enough to draw a straight line.

So if price makes:

## Low 1

and:

## Low 2

you can connect them.

But that doesn't automatically mean the market has established a meaningful trend line.

A third reaction can make the line more interesting.

---

# The Third Touch

Suppose price makes lows at:

## $50

then:

## $60

You draw a trend line.

Later price approaches that same rising line and reacts again around:

## $70

Now the market has interacted with the area multiple times.

Traders may consider the line more significant.

---

# More Touches Can Increase Attention

If price repeatedly reacts around the same trend line:

- first touch
- second touch
- third touch
- fourth touch

more traders may start noticing it.

That can make the line more widely watched.

But just like support and resistance:

# **Repeated touches do not guarantee it will keep holding.**

---

# Repeated Tests Can Eventually Break the Trend Line

Imagine a rising trend line keeps supporting price.

Each time price reaches it, buyers step in.

Eventually selling pressure becomes stronger.

The market approaches the line again and breaks below it.

The trend line may no longer be providing the same structure.

This can be an early sign that the trend is:

## Weakening

or:

## Changing.

---

# A Trend-Line Break Does Not Automatically Reverse the Trend

This is extremely important.

Suppose Bitcoin has been rising for months.

Price briefly falls below a trend line.

Does that automatically mean:

## “Bear market starts now”?

No.

A trend line is only one piece of information.

The market could:

- recover immediately
- create a new shallower trend
- move sideways
- retest the line
- genuinely reverse

You still need broader market structure.

---

# Example of a Trend-Line Break Without a Full Reversal

Imagine:

## $50 → $70 → $60 → $80 → $70 → $90

A rising trend line connects the lows.

Price then drops slightly below that line to:

## $72

but the previous major low was:

## $70

If price recovers and makes a new high, the larger uptrend may still be intact.

The diagonal line broke.

The broader structure did not necessarily break.

---

# Market Structure Matters More Than the Drawing

This is a useful rule.

If your line says:

> “Trend broken.”

but the market is still producing:

- higher highs
- higher lows

then the broader trend may still be bullish.

The line is your drawing.

The price structure is the market.

# **Trust the market more than your drawing.**

---

# Do Not Force Trend Lines

One of the most common beginner mistakes is forcing a trend line onto a chart.

They draw a line through:

- candles
- random wicks
- unrelated highs
- unrelated lows

just to make the chart fit the idea they already have.

That is backwards.

A trend line should help describe obvious structure.

You should not have to fight the chart to make the line work.

---

# The Cleaner the Line, the Easier It Is to Understand

Imagine three major lows line up naturally.

That can create a clear trend line.

Compare that with a line that needs to:

- ignore several candles
- cut through price
- skip obvious lows
- connect unrelated points

The second line is much more subjective.

Keep it simple.

---

# Wicks or Candle Bodies?

This is a common question:

> “Should I draw the trend line through the candle bodies or the wicks?”

There is no universal rule.

Some traders prefer:

## Wick extremes

because they show the full traded range.

Others use:

## Candle bodies

because they may represent where price spent more time or closed.

Often the best approach is to think in terms of:

# **Trend-Line Zones**

rather than a mathematically perfect one-pixel line.

---

# Trend Lines Are Better Treated as Areas

Just like horizontal support and resistance, price rarely respects a trend line to the exact decimal.

The market may:

- wick slightly through it
- stop just above it
- briefly break below it
- recover immediately

So think:

## Area of interest

rather than:

## Magical diagonal wall.

---

# Rising Trend Line Example

Imagine ETH creates lows at:

## $2,000

then:

## $2,200

then:

## $2,450

A rising line connects those areas.

Later ETH falls toward the projected line around:

## $2,700

A trader may watch whether buyers appear there again.

That does not mean:

> “Buy automatically at $2,700.”

It simply identifies an area where the existing trend structure may be tested.

---

# Falling Trend Line Example

Imagine a token creates highs at:

## $10

then:

## $8

then:

## $6.50

A downward line connects those highs.

Later price rallies toward the line around:

## $5.50

A trader may watch to see whether sellers appear again.

If price breaks strongly above the line, the old downtrend may be weakening.

---

# What Is a Trend-Line Breakout?

Suppose price has remained below a falling trend line.

Then price moves above it.

This can be called a:

# **Trend-Line Breakout**

Some traders interpret this as evidence that bearish momentum may be weakening.

But again:

## Breaking the line does not guarantee a new uptrend.

---

# A Breakout Needs Context

Suppose price breaks above a falling trend line but immediately runs into major horizontal resistance.

The breakout may fail.

Or perhaps price breaks the line while:

- volume increases
- resistance also breaks
- a higher low forms
- a higher high follows

That gives much more context.

---

# Fake Trend-Line Breaks

Just like horizontal support and resistance, trend lines can experience:

# **Fake Breakouts**

Imagine price has stayed below a falling trend line.

It briefly moves above.

Traders buy.

Then price immediately falls back under the line.

The breakout failed.

---

# Fake Breakdown

The same thing can happen with a rising trend line.

Price dips below the line.

Stop losses trigger.

Traders panic.

Then price quickly recovers and continues upward.

This is why one brief move through a line should not automatically be treated as a full trend reversal.

---

# Candle Closes Can Help

Some traders wait for a candle to:

## Close

beyond a trend line instead of reacting to a wick.

For example:

Price briefly wicks below a rising trend line but closes back above.

That may be treated differently from a candle that closes decisively below the line.

Again, there is no perfect confirmation system.

---

# Trend-Line Retests

After a breakout, price may return to the old trend line.

This is called a:

# **Retest**

Example:

Price stays below a falling trend line.

Then breaks above.

Later it falls back toward the old line.

If buyers appear there, the previous resistance line may now act as support.

---

# Role Reversal Can Happen With Trend Lines Too

Remember:

## Resistance can become support.

## Support can become resistance.

A similar idea can occur with trend lines.

A falling resistance trend line that breaks may later act as support.

A rising support trend line that breaks may later act as resistance.

---

# Trend Line vs Horizontal Support

Suppose a market has:

## Horizontal support at $100

and:

## Rising trend-line support near $100

at the same time.

Now two different pieces of chart structure are pointing toward the same area.

This can create:

# **Confluence**

---

# Confluence Makes an Area More Interesting

Imagine the same area contains:

- rising trend line
- previous horizontal support
- major round number
- previous swing low

That area may attract more attention than a random point on the chart.

It still does not guarantee a bounce.

But multiple pieces of market structure are lining up.

---

# What Is a Price Channel?

A:

# **Channel**

forms when price moves between two roughly parallel trend lines.

For example:

- lower line connects rising lows
- upper line connects rising highs

Price travels between them.

This creates an:

# **Ascending Channel**

---

# Ascending Channel

Imagine price moves:

## $50 → $60 → $55 → $70 → $62 → $80

The lows keep rising.

The highs keep rising.

A lower trend line can connect the higher lows.

A roughly parallel upper line can connect the higher highs.

Price is moving upward inside a channel.

---

# Descending Channel

The opposite is a:

# **Descending Channel**

Price creates:

- lower highs
- lower lows

Two downward-sloping parallel lines can frame the movement.

---

# Horizontal Channel

If the boundaries are roughly flat rather than angled, the market may simply be:

# **Range-Bound**

For example:

Support:

## $80

Resistance:

## $100

Price moves repeatedly between them.

That is essentially a horizontal channel or range.

---

# Channels Can Help Show Where Price Sits Within a Trend

Suppose price is inside an ascending channel.

Near the lower boundary:

## price is near dynamic support.

Near the upper boundary:

## price is near dynamic resistance.

Some traders use this information to assess:

- entries
- exits
- risk
- targets

But channels can break just like every other chart structure.

---

# Don't Assume Price Must Travel to the Other Side of the Channel

Imagine price touches the lower channel boundary.

It does not automatically mean:

> “Price must now reach the upper boundary.”

The market could:

- bounce halfway
- move sideways
- break downward

Channels describe previous behaviour.

They do not command future movement.

---

# Steep Trend Lines Are Often Fragile

Imagine a token suddenly pumps:

## $1 → $2 → $4 → $8

A very steep rising trend line can be drawn beneath that move.

But maintaining that rate of increase may be unrealistic.

Even a healthy slowdown could break the trend line.

That doesn't necessarily mean the asset has entered a major bearish trend.

It may simply mean:

## The rate of ascent changed.

---

# Trend Lines Can Change Angle

Markets do not always follow one perfect slope forever.

An uptrend might begin steeply.

Then slow down.

A new shallower trend line may form.

Or the opposite:

Momentum increases and a steeper trend develops.

This is why forcing price to respect an old line indefinitely is a mistake.

---

# Trend Acceleration

Imagine the market starts with lows at:

## $50

## $55

## $60

Then momentum increases:

## $70

## $85

## $105

The slope of the trend may become steeper.

This is:

## Trend acceleration.

Strong acceleration can be bullish.

But extremely steep movement can also become unstable.

---

# Trend Deceleration

The reverse can happen.

Price keeps rising, but each new high gains less distance.

The upward trend becomes shallower.

This may suggest:

## Momentum is weakening.

It does not guarantee reversal.

But the behaviour is changing.

---

# Trend Lines and Timeframe

A trend line on a:

## 5-minute chart

may last for an hour.

A trend line on a:

## weekly chart

could remain relevant for years.

Always know which timeframe you're analysing.

---

# Higher-Timeframe Trend Lines

Trend lines drawn from major weekly or monthly swing points may attract significant attention because they represent longer periods of price history.

However:

## Higher timeframe does not mean unbreakable.

Every trend eventually changes.

---

# Lower-Timeframe Trend Lines

Short-term traders may use:

- 1-minute
- 5-minute
- 15-minute

trend lines.

These can help analyse very short-term structure.

But they are often more vulnerable to:

## Noise.

---

# Trend Lines on Low-Liquidity Tokens

Be careful.

Low-liquidity tokens can produce erratic:

- spikes
- wicks
- gaps
- sudden reversals

One medium-sized trade can completely distort a trend line.

Technical structure tends to become harder to interpret when liquidity is extremely thin.

---

# One Whale Can Destroy the Trend Line

Imagine a tiny token has been slowly rising.

The chart has a beautiful upward trend line.

Then one large holder sells.

Price drops:

## 40%

The line is instantly destroyed.

The trend line was not protection.

It was only a visual representation of previous trading behaviour.

---

# Trend Lines on Different Exchanges Can Differ

A token trading on multiple exchanges may have slightly different:

- highs
- lows
- wicks
- volume

Therefore:

## Trend lines may not be identical on every venue.

This is especially true for less liquid assets.

---

# DEX Pools Can Produce Different Structures Too

One TOKEN/USDC pool might have different price history from another pool.

Arbitrage can bring prices closer together.

But each pool still has its own:

- trades
- reserves
- liquidity
- candle data

Always know which market your chart represents.

---

# Trend Lines and Logarithmic Charts

On long-term charts, traders sometimes switch from:

## Linear scale

to:

## Logarithmic scale.

This can change how trend lines appear.

Why?

Because logarithmic charts emphasise:

## Percentage movement

rather than equal dollar movement.

---

# Example

Moving from:

## $1 → $2

is:

## +100%

Moving from:

## $100 → $101

is:

## +1%

A log chart treats percentage changes more consistently.

This can be useful when analysing assets that have increased dramatically over many years.

---

# Trend Lines Are Subjective

Two traders can look at the same chart and draw slightly different trend lines.

One might use:

## Wick lows.

Another:

## Candle closes.

One might start from an older swing.

Another from a newer structure.

Technical analysis contains interpretation.

Do not assume your exact line is the only possible correct one.

---

# A Trend Line Should Explain Price — Not Hide It

A useful line makes the chart easier to understand.

If drawing it makes the chart more confusing:

## Remove it.

The goal is clarity.

Not decoration.

---

# Avoid Charts Covered in Trend Lines

Beginners sometimes create charts with:

- 15 rising lines
- 12 falling lines
- multiple channels
- dozens of support zones

Eventually price is touching something everywhere.

That doesn't improve analysis.

It creates noise.

---

# Start With the Most Obvious Structure

A beginner can focus on:

## 1. Major swing lows

## 2. Major swing highs

## 3. Clear rising or falling structure

## 4. The most obvious trend line

Keep the chart readable.

---

# Trend Lines Can Help With Stop Placement

Suppose you buy inside a rising trend.

Your trade idea is:

> “I expect the rising structure to continue.”

If price clearly breaks below:

- the trend line
- previous higher low
- horizontal support

the trade thesis may become weaker.

These areas can help define where a stop might make sense.

---

# Don't Put a Stop Exactly on the Trend Line Without Thinking

Obvious levels often attract:

- stop orders
- breakout orders
- liquidity

Price can briefly wick through a trend line before recovering.

Placing stops mechanically on the exact line may expose you to ordinary market noise.

Risk planning should consider:

- volatility
- liquidity
- market structure

not simply the pixel position of your drawing.

---

# Trend Lines Can Help With Profit Targets

Suppose price is trading inside an ascending channel.

A trader enters near the lower portion.

They may watch the upper channel boundary as a potential area to:

- take profit
- reduce exposure
- tighten risk

Again, it is a planning tool.

Not a guaranteed target.

---

# A Break of a Steep Trend Line Can Be Healthy

Suppose Bitcoin rises extremely quickly.

The trend line becomes almost vertical.

Price eventually breaks below it and consolidates.

A beginner might think:

> “Trend destroyed.”

But the market could simply be slowing from:

## Unsustainably fast growth

to:

## More gradual growth.

The larger uptrend can remain intact.

---

# Trend Line vs Trend

This distinction is critical.

# **Trend Line**

A drawing used to visualise price structure.

# **Trend**

The actual direction and structure of the market.

A trend line can break while the broader trend continues.

Never confuse the drawing with the market itself.

---

# Common Beginner Trend-Line Mistakes

## 1. Forcing a Line Onto the Chart

If the price structure doesn't naturally fit, don't force it.

---

## 2. Treating the Line as Exact

Think in areas, not one perfect pixel.

---

## 3. Using Only Two Weak Points

Two random points can create almost any line.

Look for meaningful swing points and additional confirmation.

---

## 4. Ignoring Market Structure

A line breaking doesn't automatically reverse higher highs and higher lows.

---

## 5. Drawing Through Lots of Price Action

If the line constantly cuts through candles, it may not be useful.

---

## 6. Ignoring Timeframe

A five-minute trend line is not the same as a weekly one.

---

## 7. Assuming Every Touch Will Bounce

Trend lines can fail.

---

## 8. Buying Every Breakout

Fake breakouts happen.

---

## 9. Using Too Many Lines

Keep the chart readable.

---

## 10. Forgetting Liquidity

Low-liquidity markets can destroy technical structure very quickly.

---

# A Better Beginner Routine

When drawing a trend line, ask:

## 1. Is the market actually trending?

## 2. Am I connecting meaningful swing highs or lows?

## 3. Does the line naturally fit the chart?

## 4. Has price interacted with it multiple times?

## 5. What timeframe am I using?

## 6. Is horizontal support or resistance nearby?

## 7. Is volume supporting the move?

## 8. Did price merely wick through the line or close beyond it?

## 9. Has the broader market structure changed?

## 10. Am I using the line as information rather than treating it as a guarantee?

That keeps the trend line in its proper role.

Price moves:

## $50 → $70

pulls back:

## $70 → $60

rallies:

## $60 → $85

pulls back:

## $85 → $70

then rallies:

## $70 → $100

The lows are:

## $50

## $60

## $70

A rising line connects them.

At the same time, price is creating:

## Higher highs

and:

## Higher lows.

That is much stronger evidence of an uptrend than the trend line alone.

# Simple Downtrend Example

Price moves:

## $100 → $70

rallies:

## $70 → $90

falls:

## $90 → $60

rallies:

## $60 → $80

then falls:

## $80 → $50

The highs are:

## $100

## $90

## $80

A falling line connects them.

The broader market is also creating:

## Lower highs

and:

## Lower lows.

The trend line and market structure agree.

# The Key Lesson

Trend lines are simple tools used to visualise directional market structure.

Remember:

### 📈 RISING TREND LINE

**Connects important higher lows and can represent dynamic support.**

### 📉 FALLING TREND LINE

**Connects important lower highs and can represent dynamic resistance.**

### 💥 TREND-LINE BREAK

**Shows price has moved through the previous trend boundary — but does not automatically confirm a complete reversal.**

### 🔄 RETEST

**Price returns to the broken line to test it from the opposite side.**

### 📐 CHANNEL

**Two roughly parallel boundaries containing price movement.**

Most importantly:

# **The trend line is your drawing. The trend is the market.**

Do not force price to fit your line.

Use the line to help understand price.

If the market changes, change your analysis with it.
