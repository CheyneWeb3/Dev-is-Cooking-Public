# **Trading Volume**

# **Liquidity**

---

# What Is Liquidity?

Liquidity describes how easily an asset can be bought or sold without causing a large change in its price.

A:

# **Highly Liquid Market**

usually allows larger trades with relatively small price movement.

A:

# **Low-Liquidity Market**

can move dramatically from a much smaller trade.

---

# Simple Liquidity Example

Imagine two tokens are both trading around:

## $1

### Token A

There are millions of dollars of buy and sell orders close to $1.

### Token B

There are only a few thousand dollars of orders near $1.

If someone market buys:

## $50,000

Token A might barely move.

Token B could move dramatically.

Same displayed price.

Very different liquidity.

---

# Why Liquidity Matters

Liquidity affects:

- how easily you can enter a trade
- how easily you can exit
- slippage
- price impact
- spreads
- volatility
- execution quality

A token showing a good price means very little if there is almost no liquidity behind that price.

---

# What Is an Order Book?

An:

# **Order Book**

is a live list of buy and sell orders waiting to be executed on a market.

It usually has two sides:

# **Bids**

and:

# **Asks**

Think of it as buyers and sellers publicly stating the prices they are currently prepared to trade at.

---

# What Is a Bid?

A:

## **Bid**

is an offer to buy an asset at a particular price.

For example:

> Buy 100 tokens at $9.90

That trader is willing to buy:

## 100 tokens

but does not want to pay more than:

## $9.90 each.

---

# What Is an Ask?

An:

## **Ask**

is an offer to sell an asset at a particular price.

For example:

> Sell 100 tokens at $10.10

That trader is willing to sell:

## 100 tokens

if someone pays:

## $10.10 each.

---

# The Highest Bid

The:

# **Highest Bid**

is the highest price any current buyer is willing to pay.

Example:

Buyers are offering:

## $9.80

## $9.90

## $9.95

The highest bid is:

## $9.95

---

# The Lowest Ask

The:

# **Lowest Ask**

is the cheapest price any current seller is willing to accept.

Example:

Sellers are asking:

## $10.05

## $10.10

## $10.20

The lowest ask is:

## $10.05

---

# What Is the Spread?

The difference between the:

## Highest bid

and:

## Lowest ask

is called the:

# **Bid-Ask Spread**

In our example:

Highest bid:

## $9.95

Lowest ask:

## $10.05

Spread:

## $0.10

---

# Tight Spread vs Wide Spread

A:

## Tight Spread

means buyers and sellers are quoting prices close together.

For example:

Bid:

## $99.99

Ask:

## $100.01

Spread:

## $0.02

A:

## Wide Spread

means there is a larger gap.

Example:

Bid:

## $90

Ask:

## $100

Spread:

## $10

Wide spreads are often associated with less liquid markets.

---

# Why Tight Spreads Matter

If you want to buy and immediately sell in a tight market, the difference between the two prices may be relatively small.

In a wide market, you could lose a significant amount simply crossing the spread.

Example:

You market buy at:

## $100

The highest immediate buyer is only:

## $90

If you immediately market sell, you could lose around:

## 10%

before even considering fees.

---

# Order Book Example

Imagine TOKEN X has this order book:

## Sell Orders — Asks

| Price | Amount |
|---:|---:|
| $10.20 | 500 |
| $10.10 | 300 |
| $10.05 | 100 |

## Buy Orders — Bids

| Price | Amount |
|---:|---:|
| $9.95 | 200 |
| $9.90 | 400 |
| $9.80 | 1,000 |

The best prices are:

## Best Ask: $10.05

## Best Bid: $9.95

The spread is:

## $0.10

---

# What Happens When You Market Buy?

Suppose you place a market order to buy:

## 50 tokens

The cheapest seller is offering:

## 100 tokens at $10.05

Your entire order can fill at:

## $10.05

Easy.

---

# What If You Buy More?

Now suppose you want:

## 500 tokens

Available asks are:

- 100 at $10.05
- 300 at $10.10
- 500 at $10.20

Your order consumes:

## 100 at $10.05

then:

## 300 at $10.10

then:

## 100 at $10.20

Your average execution price is now higher than the original best ask.

This is how a large market order:

# **Walks the Order Book**

---

# Walking the Order Book

When a market order is larger than the amount available at the best price, it starts consuming orders at progressively worse prices.

For a market buy:

## It moves upward through the asks.

For a market sell:

## It moves downward through the bids.

The larger your order relative to available liquidity:

# **The more slippage you may experience.**

---

# Simple Market Buy Calculation

Available sellers:

100 tokens at:

## $10.00

200 tokens at:

## $10.10

500 tokens at:

## $10.30

You want:

## 500 tokens.

Your purchase becomes:

100 × $10.00 =

## $1,000

200 × $10.10 =

## $2,020

200 × $10.30 =

## $2,060

Total:

## $5,080

Average execution:

## $10.16 per token

The displayed best price was:

## $10.00

But your actual average price was:

## $10.16

because your order consumed several levels of liquidity.

---

# The Same Happens When Selling

Imagine buyers are waiting at:

100 tokens:

## $10

200 tokens:

## $9.90

500 tokens:

## $9.50

You market sell:

## 500 tokens.

Your sale moves downward through those bids.

You do not necessarily receive:

## $10 for every token.

Your average execution price becomes lower.

---

# This Is Why “The Price” Can Be Misleading

A platform might display:

# TOKEN X = $10

But that does not mean:

> “Every TOKEN X I own can be sold for $10.”

The displayed price may reflect:

- last trade
- midpoint
- best bid/ask
- another reference price

Your actual execution depends on:

# **Available liquidity**

---

# Example: Million-Dollar Position That Isn't Really Worth $1 Million at Exit

Suppose you own:

## 1,000,000 tokens

The displayed token price is:

## $1

Your wallet appears to contain:

## $1,000,000

worth of tokens.

But the order book might only have:

## $20,000

of buyers close to $1.

Selling your entire position could push the price down dramatically.

You may receive much less than:

## $1 million.

This is why:

# **Displayed value ≠ guaranteed exit value**

---

# Market Value Is Based on Marginal Price

The displayed price often represents the price of:

## the most recent or next small trade.

That price can then be multiplied across your entire token balance.

But your whole position cannot necessarily be sold at that marginal price.

This distinction is especially important in:

- small-cap tokens
- new listings
- thin markets
- DEX pools

---

# What Is Order-Book Depth?

# **Order-Book Depth**

describes how much buying and selling liquidity exists across different prices.

A deep order book might have:

- large buy orders
- large sell orders
- many price levels
- tight spreads

A shallow order book may have:

- small orders
- large price gaps
- little depth

---

# Deep Market Example

Around the current price of:

## $100

there may be:

- $1 million of bids between $99–$100
- $1 million of asks between $100–$101

A $5,000 market order is relatively tiny compared with available liquidity.

Price impact may be very small.

---

# Shallow Market Example

Current price:

## $100

But there may only be:

## $1,000 of asks

before the next sellers appear at:

## $110.

A $10,000 market buy could move the market considerably.

---

# Depth Is Often Displayed Visually

Some exchanges provide a:

# **Depth Chart**

This graph shows cumulative:

- buy orders
- sell orders

at different prices.

One side represents bids.

The other represents asks.

This helps traders see how much liquidity is stacked around the current market price.

---

# What Is a Buy Wall?

A:

# **Buy Wall**

is a very large concentration of buy orders around a particular price.

Example:

Normal bids:

## $10,000 each

Then suddenly:

## $1 million buy order at $90

That might appear as a large wall in the order book.

Traders may interpret it as potential support.

But there is a catch.

---

# Buy Walls Can Disappear

A limit order sitting in an order book has not executed yet.

The trader may be able to:

## Cancel it.

That $1 million buy wall could disappear seconds before price reaches it.

So:

# **Visible order-book liquidity is not guaranteed future liquidity.**

---

# What Is a Sell Wall?

A:

# **Sell Wall**

is a large amount of sell orders clustered around a price.

Example:

Current price:

## $90

Huge sell orders:

## $100

Traders may see that as resistance.

But just like a buy wall:

## The orders can potentially be cancelled.

---

# Order Books Show Intentions, Not Guarantees

An order book tells you:

> “These orders are currently being offered.”

It does not tell you:

> “These orders will definitely still be here when price arrives.”

That distinction matters.

---

# What Is Spoofing?

# **Spoofing**

generally refers to placing large orders with the intention of creating a misleading impression of demand or supply and cancelling them before execution.

For example:

A trader displays a huge buy wall to make the market appear heavily supported.

Other traders react.

The wall disappears.

Spoofing is prohibited in many regulated markets.

Crypto markets can still contain manipulative order-book behaviour.

---

# Don't Blindly Trust Walls

A huge buy wall does not mean:

## Guaranteed support.

A huge sell wall does not mean:

## Guaranteed resistance.

Look at actual trades and execution as well.

---

# Makers and Takers

Remember Lesson #50.

A trader placing a limit order that waits in the order book may be providing liquidity.

This trader is commonly called a:

# **Maker**

Someone who immediately trades against existing orders removes liquidity.

They are commonly called a:

# **Taker**

---

# Makers Add Liquidity

Suppose the current best bid is:

## $99

You place a limit buy at:

## $98.

Your order waits.

You have added another potential buyer to the order book.

That contributes to market liquidity.

---

# Takers Remove Liquidity

Now someone uses a market sell.

Their order consumes available bids.

They are removing liquidity from the order book.

This is why market orders are often described as:

# **Taking liquidity**

---

# Market Orders Prioritise Execution

A market order says:

> **“Execute using whatever liquidity is available now.”**

That is why market orders can walk through multiple price levels.

The order does not wait for your ideal price.

---

# Limit Orders Prioritise Price

A limit order says:

> **“Only execute at this price or better.”**

If the necessary liquidity is not available:

## The order waits.

This is why limit orders can add liquidity rather than immediately consume it.

---

# Liquidity Changes Constantly

An order book is not static.

Orders are continuously:

- added
- cancelled
- filled
- modified

The liquidity you see now may look completely different:

## one second later.

This is particularly true in fast electronic markets.

---

# Trading Bots Update Order Books Constantly

Market makers and automated trading systems can place and cancel orders extremely quickly.

They may adjust prices based on:

- other exchanges
- volatility
- inventory
- market movements

So the order book is constantly moving.

---

# Why Thin Markets Move So Fast

Imagine only:

## $5,000

of sell liquidity exists between:

## $1 and $2.

Someone places a:

## $50,000 market buy.

The order consumes all available sellers near $1.

Then $1.10.

Then $1.20.

Then $1.50.

Potentially much higher.

The market can move dramatically because there simply wasn't enough liquidity available.

---

# Price Movement Is About Liquidity, Not Just Trade Size

A:

## $10,000 trade

might barely move Bitcoin.

The same:

## $10,000

could move an obscure token by:

## 50%.

The dollar amount is identical.

The available liquidity is not.

---

# This Is Why Small Tokens Can Pump So Easily

Suppose a new token has:

## very little sell liquidity.

A few buyers arrive.

Each market order consumes the next available sellers.

Price moves:

## $0.10 → $0.15 → $0.30 → $0.50

The percentage gain looks enormous.

But perhaps only a relatively small amount of capital was needed to move the market.

---

# The Reverse Is Also True

If buyers disappear, one large seller may push the token:

## $0.50 → $0.30 → $0.15 → $0.05

very quickly.

Low liquidity works in:

# **Both directions**

---

# Liquidity and Volatility Are Connected

Thin markets often experience greater:

## Volatility

because relatively small trades can move price substantially.

Deep markets can absorb more trading before price moves dramatically.

This is one reason major markets tend to behave differently from tiny tokens.

---

# Liquidity Can Disappear During Panic

A market might normally look liquid.

Then major news hits.

Market makers may:

- cancel orders
- widen spreads
- reduce position sizes

Suddenly the order book becomes much thinner.

This is called:

## Liquidity drying up.

---

# Example

Normal market:

Bid:

## $99.99

Ask:

## $100.01

During panic:

Bid:

## $95

Ask:

## $105

The spread exploded.

The market became much harder to trade efficiently.

---

# This Is Why Stops Can Slip During Crashes

Suppose your stop triggers around:

## $100

But the next available bids are:

## $99

## $95

## $90

Your stop-market order may fill across those lower prices.

The stop triggered correctly.

Liquidity was the problem.

---

# Liquidity and Slippage

Remember:

# **Slippage**

is the difference between the expected execution price and the actual price received.

Low liquidity generally increases the risk of slippage.

Large orders relative to available depth also increase it.

---

# Liquidity and Price Impact

Price impact describes how much your own trade changes the market.

A large trade in a shallow order book can remove several price levels.

The result:

## Your own order pushes price against you.

---

# Spread, Slippage and Price Impact Are Related but Different

### Spread

Difference between best bid and best ask.

### Slippage

Difference between expected and actual execution.

### Price Impact

How much your trade itself moves through available liquidity.

They often interact.

But they are not identical.

---

# Order Books Are Common on Centralised Exchanges

Most traditional crypto exchanges use:

# **Central Limit Order Books**

Buyers and sellers submit orders.

The exchange's matching engine pairs compatible orders.

This is the classic:

## Bid/Ask system.

---

# But Many DEXs Do Not Use Order Books

This is important for crypto.

A classic automated market maker DEX may use:

# **Liquidity Pools**

instead of a traditional order book.

For example:

## TOKEN A / USDC

The price is determined by the relationship between assets in the pool.

There may be no visible list of bids and asks.

---

# Order-Book Liquidity vs Pool Liquidity

Both systems provide markets.

But they work differently.

### Order Book

Liquidity comes from:

## Buy and sell orders at specific prices.

### AMM Liquidity Pool

Liquidity comes from:

## Assets deposited into a smart contract.

Traders swap against those reserves.

---

# DEX Liquidity Example

Imagine a pool contains:

## 100,000 TOKEN

and:

## 100,000 USDC

The market begins around:

## $1 per TOKEN

depending on the AMM formula.

If someone buys a large amount of TOKEN:

- TOKEN leaves the pool
- USDC enters
- the reserve ratio changes
- the trading price rises

The pool itself effectively provides the liquidity curve.

---

# DEX Liquidity Can Still Be Deep or Shallow

A pool containing:

## $50 million

of liquidity may handle large trades relatively well.

A pool containing:

## $5,000

may move dramatically from a relatively small swap.

The same liquidity principles still apply.

---

# Concentrated Liquidity

More advanced AMMs can use:

# **Concentrated Liquidity**

Liquidity providers choose specific price ranges where their capital is active.

This can create very deep liquidity around certain prices but much less liquidity outside those ranges.

So even:

## Total Value Locked

does not always tell you how much active liquidity exists at the current price.

---

# Liquidity Can Be Fragmented Across Markets

A token may trade on:

- Exchange A
- Exchange B
- DEX A
- DEX B
- several liquidity pools

Each market has separate liquidity.

You cannot automatically treat all of it as one giant pool.

---

# Arbitrage Links Separate Markets

Suppose:

TOKEN X:

## $10 on Exchange A

and:

## $11 on Exchange B.

Arbitrage traders may:

- buy at $10
- sell at $11

This trading helps bring prices closer together.

But each exchange still maintains its own actual liquidity.

---

# Why Two Exchanges Can Show Different Prices

If markets are separate, temporary differences can appear because of:

- different liquidity
- different orders
- different traders
- network delays
- withdrawal restrictions

Arbitrage usually helps reduce the difference when it is practical to trade between them.

---

# Order-Book Depth and Arbitrage

Suppose Exchange A shows:

## $10

Exchange B shows:

## $11

That looks like:

## $1 profit per token.

But imagine only:

## 5 tokens

are available at $10.

After those are bought, the next ask is:

## $10.90.

The apparent arbitrage almost disappears.

This is why professional traders care about:

# **Executable depth**

not just the displayed top price.

---

# The Best Bid and Ask Only Show the First Layer

The current quote might be:

## $9.99 / $10.01

That looks extremely liquid.

But perhaps only:

## $5 worth

exists at those prices.

The next level might be much worse.

Always consider:

## How much size is actually available.

---

# This Is Called Market Depth

The deeper the available liquidity across nearby prices:

## The more size the market can absorb.

Top-of-book price alone is not enough.

---

# Why a Whale Cares About Depth

A trader buying:

## $100

may barely care.

A trader buying:

## $10 million

needs to know:

- how much liquidity exists
- how quickly it can replenish
- how much price impact the order creates

Large traders cannot simply assume the displayed price applies to their whole order.

---

# Splitting Large Orders

Large traders may split an order into smaller pieces rather than executing everything at once.

This can reduce immediate:

## Market impact.

Algorithms may execute gradually over time.

This does not eliminate risk.

But it can avoid smashing through the entire order book at once.

---

# Hidden and Iceberg Orders

Some markets support orders where the full size is not visible publicly.

An:

# **Iceberg Order**

may display only a portion of a larger order.

As visible quantity fills, more becomes available.

This means:

## The visible order book may not show every piece of liquidity.

---

# Not All Liquidity Is Visible

Other hidden-order mechanisms and off-book liquidity can also exist depending on the venue.

So the visible order book is useful.

But it may not represent every possible trade source.

---

# Order Book Does Not Equal Trading Volume

Remember Lesson #62.

### Order Book

Shows:

## Orders currently waiting.

### Volume

Shows:

## Trades that actually occurred.

A huge order sitting in the book generates:

## Zero trading volume

until someone trades against it.

---

# Example

A $10 million sell wall sits at:

## $100

for one hour.

Nobody buys it.

Trading volume from that wall:

## $0

It is available liquidity, not completed volume.

---

# Orders Can Be Cancelled Before Becoming Volume

This is another reason volume and order-book depth are different.

An order can appear:

## huge

then disappear without one token changing hands.

---

# Liquidity and Market Cap Are Different Too

Suppose:

Token supply:

## 100 million

Price:

## $1

Market cap:

## $100 million

But its active trading liquidity might only be:

## $500,000.

The token can have a $100 million calculated market cap while being extremely difficult to sell in size.

---

# Market Cap Does Not Guarantee Exit Liquidity

This is a crucial crypto lesson.

A token might be advertised as:

## “$500 million market cap.”

That does not mean:

## $500 million is sitting somewhere ready to buy holders out.

Market cap is a valuation calculation.

Liquidity is actual trading capacity.

---

# Fully Diluted Valuation Doesn't Equal Liquidity Either

FDV may be even larger.

Example:

Total supply × price:

## $1 billion FDV

Liquidity:

## $1 million

Those figures describe completely different things.

---

# Why Rug Pulls Destroy Liquidity

On some DEX tokens, the team or liquidity provider may control a large share of the pool liquidity.

If that liquidity is removed:

## Traders may suddenly have almost nowhere to sell.

Price can collapse.

This is one reason checking liquidity ownership and lock conditions can matter for small tokens.

---

# A Token Can Still Exist With Almost No Market

The smart contract may still exist.

Your wallet may still show:

## 1 million tokens.

A price tracker may even show a previous price.

But if there are no meaningful buyers or liquidity:

## Your tokens may be effectively unsellable.

Ownership does not guarantee a market.

---

# Liquidity Is What Makes an Exit Possible

This is probably the most important practical lesson.

When you buy an asset, don't only ask:

> “Can the price go up?”

Also ask:

# **“Who can I sell to later, and how much liquidity is available?”**

An entry is easy to understand.

An exit is what turns displayed value into realisable value.

---

# Common Beginner Liquidity Mistakes

## 1. Looking Only at the Displayed Price

The whole position may not be executable at that price.

---

## 2. Confusing Volume With Liquidity

High historical volume does not guarantee current depth.

---

## 3. Confusing Market Cap With Liquidity

A huge valuation does not mean huge available buyers.

---

## 4. Ignoring the Spread

Wide spreads can create immediate trading costs.

---

## 5. Using Large Market Orders in Thin Markets

The order can move price dramatically.

---

## 6. Believing Buy and Sell Walls Are Guaranteed

Orders can be cancelled.

---

## 7. Ignoring Order-Book Depth

The best bid or ask is only the first price level.

---

## 8. Assuming Every DEX Uses an Order Book

Many use AMM liquidity pools instead.

---

## 9. Ignoring Fragmented Liquidity

A token may have different liquidity across multiple venues.

---

## 10. Thinking Wallet Value Equals Cash-Out Value

Real exit value depends on executable liquidity.

---

# A Better Beginner Routine

Before making a trade, ask:

## 1. What is the spread?

## 2. How much size exists at the best bid and ask?

## 3. How deep is the market nearby?

## 4. How large is my trade relative to that depth?

## 5. What slippage could I experience?

## 6. Is liquidity concentrated on one exchange or pool?

## 7. Is this an order book or AMM market?

## 8. Could visible walls disappear?

## 9. How much could I realistically sell without moving price heavily?

## 10. What happens if liquidity suddenly disappears?

Those questions can matter just as much as:

> “Do I think the token goes up?”

Order book:

## $500

Your trade is tiny relative to market depth.

Expected price impact:

## Very small.

Current market:

## $100

Order book:

- $500 available at $100

- next seller at $125

Your order:

## $10,000

The displayed:

## $100

did not represent the price available for your entire order.

# The Key Lesson

Liquidity tells you how easily a market can absorb buying and selling.

An order book shows where buyers and sellers are currently willing to trade.

Remember:

### 🟢 BID

**An order offering to buy.**

### 🔴 ASK

**An order offering to sell.**

### ↔️ SPREAD

**The gap between the highest bid and lowest ask.**

### 📚 ORDER-BOOK DEPTH

**The amount of liquidity available across different price levels.**

### 💧 LIQUIDITY

**How easily an asset can be traded without causing major price movement.**

### ⚡ MARKET ORDER

**Consumes available liquidity immediately.**

Most importantly:

# **The displayed price is not necessarily the price at which you can sell your entire position.**

Price tells you where the market is trading.

Liquidity tells you whether you can actually trade meaningful size there.

Before asking:

> “How much is my token worth?”

also ask:

# **“How much can I realistically sell at that price?”**

That's a much more useful question.
