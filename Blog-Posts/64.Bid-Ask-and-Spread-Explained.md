# **Liquidity and Order Books**

We learned that an order book contains:

- buyers waiting to buy
- sellers waiting to sell
- different quantities available at different prices

Now we're going to zoom right into the centre of that order book.

Three terms you'll see constantly are:

# **Bid**

# **Ask**

and:

# **Spread**

Understanding these three numbers helps explain why the price shown on a screen isn't always the exact price you'll receive when you trade.

So let's cook it down.

---

# What Is the Bid?

The:

## **Bid**

is the price a buyer is currently offering to pay for an asset.

Suppose someone places an order saying:

> “I'll buy 100 tokens at $9.90 each.”

That:

## $9.90

is a bid.

There can be many different bids sitting in an order book at the same time.

---

# What Is the Best Bid?

The:

# **Best Bid**

is the highest current price a buyer is willing to pay.

Imagine the buy side of an order book contains:

| Buyer | Bid Price | Amount |
|---|---:|---:|
| Buyer A | $9.80 | 500 |
| Buyer B | $9.90 | 200 |
| Buyer C | $9.95 | 100 |

The highest bid is:

## $9.95

So:

# **Best Bid = $9.95**

If you wanted to immediately sell a small amount using a market order, this may be the first available price your sale hits.

---

# What Is the Ask?

The:

## **Ask**

is the price a seller is currently asking for an asset.

For example:

> “I'll sell 100 tokens for $10.05 each.”

That:

## $10.05

is an ask.

Like bids, there can be many asks at different prices and amounts.

---

# What Is the Best Ask?

The:

# **Best Ask**

is the lowest current price a seller is willing to accept.

Imagine:

| Seller | Ask Price | Amount |
|---|---:|---:|
| Seller A | $10.20 | 500 |
| Seller B | $10.10 | 200 |
| Seller C | $10.05 | 100 |

The cheapest seller is:

## $10.05

So:

# **Best Ask = $10.05**

If you immediately market buy a small amount, this may be the first price you pay.

---

# Bid vs Ask

The easiest way to remember them:

### 🟢 Bid

**What buyers are offering.**

### 🔴 Ask

**What sellers are asking.**

Or even simpler:

# Bid = Buy Side

# Ask = Sell Side

---

# What Is the Spread?

The:

# **Bid-Ask Spread**

is the difference between the best bid and best ask.

Suppose:

Best Bid:

## $9.95

Best Ask:

## $10.05

Spread:

## $10.05 - $9.95 = $0.10

So the market has a:

# **$0.10 spread**

---

# Spread as a Percentage

You can also express the spread as a percentage.

Suppose:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

The difference is:

## $2

Using a price around $100 as the reference, that spread is approximately:

## 2%

The wider the percentage spread:

## The more expensive immediate trading can become.

---

# Tight Spread

A:

# **Tight Spread**

means buyers and sellers are quoting prices very close together.

Example:

Bid:

## $99.99

Ask:

## $100.01

Spread:

## $0.02

This often occurs in highly liquid markets.

---

# Wide Spread

A:

# **Wide Spread**

means there is a much larger difference between the highest buyer and cheapest seller.

Example:

Bid:

## $90

Ask:

## $100

Spread:

## $10

That is a very different market.

Someone buying and immediately selling could take a significant loss simply from crossing the spread.

---

# Why Does the Spread Exist?

Buyers naturally want:

## Lower prices.

Sellers naturally want:

## Higher prices.

The spread is the gap between what the most competitive buyer is currently willing to pay and what the most competitive seller is currently willing to accept.

When one side crosses that gap:

## A trade occurs.

---

# How Does a Trade Happen?

Suppose:

Best Bid:

## $9.95

Best Ask:

## $10.05

A buyer places a market order.

They accept the seller's:

## $10.05 ask.

Trade occurs.

Alternatively, a seller places a market sell.

They accept the buyer's:

## $9.95 bid.

Trade occurs.

---

# Market Orders Cross the Spread

This is important.

A:

## Market Buy

typically crosses the spread and trades against the ask side.

A:

## Market Sell

typically crosses the spread and trades against the bid side.

This creates an immediate execution cost.

---

# Simple Example

Suppose:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

You market buy one token.

You pay approximately:

## $101

Immediately afterwards, you market sell it.

The best buyer is:

## $99

Ignoring fees, you just lost:

## $2

even though the broader market may not have moved at all.

That loss came from:

# **Crossing the spread twice**

---

# Spread Is a Hidden Trading Cost

People often focus only on:

## Exchange fees.

But the spread can also act like a trading cost.

Imagine:

Exchange fee:

## 0.1%

Spread:

## 3%

The spread may matter far more than the advertised exchange fee.

This is especially common in illiquid markets.

---

# Why Major Markets Usually Have Tight Spreads

Markets such as major BTC or ETH pairs often contain:

- many buyers
- many sellers
- market makers
- trading bots
- large amounts of liquidity

These participants compete with each other.

One seller might ask:

## $100.10

Another offers:

## $100.05

Another:

## $100.01

Competition pushes the best prices closer together.

---

# Why Small Tokens Often Have Wide Spreads

A small token might have very few active participants.

Imagine:

Highest buyer:

## $0.80

Cheapest seller:

## $1.00

That's a:

## $0.20 gap

on a token worth roughly $1.

Approximately:

## 20%

That is enormous.

---

# A Wide Spread Can Signal Low Liquidity

A wide spread can indicate:

- fewer traders
- less market-making activity
- poor order-book depth
- uncertainty
- volatility

It does not always mean something is wrong.

But it deserves attention.

---

# The Chart Price May Not Be the Price You Can Buy At

Suppose the chart displays:

# TOKEN X = $10

But the order book shows:

Best Bid:

## $9.50

Best Ask:

## $10.50

What does:

## $10

actually mean?

It might be:

- last traded price
- midpoint
- index price
- mark price
- another reference value

If you want to buy immediately:

## You might actually pay $10.50.

If you want to sell immediately:

## You might only receive $9.50.

---

# What Is the Last Price?

The:

# **Last Price**

is the price of the most recent completed trade.

Suppose the last transaction happened at:

## $10

The screen may show:

## TOKEN = $10

But current buyers and sellers may already be quoting:

Bid:

## $9.80

Ask:

## $10.20

The last price describes:

## What just happened.

The bid and ask describe:

## What is currently available.

---

# What Is the Mid Price?

The:

# **Mid Price**

is halfway between the best bid and best ask.

Example:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

Midpoint:

## $100

Calculation:

## ($99 + $101) ÷ 2 = $100

Some platforms may use a midpoint for displaying market information.

But there may not actually be an order available at exactly $100.

---

# Mid Price Is Not Necessarily Executable

This is important.

Bid:

## $90

Ask:

## $110

Midpoint:

## $100

The screen might suggest:

## $100

as a central reference price.

But there is:

## No buyer at $100

and:

## No seller at $100.

You could immediately sell around $90 or buy around $110.

The midpoint is just a calculation.

---

# Best Bid and Best Ask Are Called the Top of Book

The most competitive buy and sell prices are often referred to as:

# **Top of Book**

That means:

- highest bid
- lowest ask

These are the first orders a small market order will generally interact with.

---

# Top of Book Does Not Tell You Everything

Suppose:

Best Ask:

## $10

Amount available:

## 2 tokens

Next ask:

## $12

Amount:

## 1,000 tokens

The displayed best ask is:

## $10

But only two tokens are actually available there.

A larger order would quickly reach:

## $12.

This is why Lesson #63's concept of:

# **Order-Book Depth**

still matters.

---

# Spread vs Slippage

These two terms are often confused.

## Spread

The gap between:

## Best Bid and Best Ask.

## Slippage

The difference between the price you expected and the average price you actually received.

You can experience both on the same trade.

---

# Example: Spread Plus Slippage

Order book:

Best Ask:

## $10

Only:

## 10 tokens available.

Next asks:

## $10.20

## $10.50

You want:

## 1,000 tokens.

You already cross the spread when buying.

Then your large order consumes several higher ask levels.

Now you experience:

## additional slippage.

---

# Spread vs Price Impact

Price impact refers to how much your trade changes the available market price because it consumes liquidity.

Suppose your large market buy removes sellers at:

## $10

## $10.10

## $10.20

After your order, the best remaining seller is:

## $10.50

Your trade itself changed the visible market.

That is price impact.

---

# Spread, Slippage and Price Impact Together

Think of it like this:

### Spread

The gap already exists before you trade.

### Slippage

The difference between what you expected and what you actually receive.

### Price Impact

The effect your own order has on available prices.

These can all contribute to execution cost.

---

# Market Makers Help Create the Spread

A:

# **Market Maker**

continuously posts both buy and sell orders.

For example:

Buy:

## $99.95

Sell:

## $100.05

The market maker provides liquidity on both sides.

If they can repeatedly buy slightly lower and sell slightly higher, the spread can contribute to their revenue.

---

# Market Makers Compete

Suppose Market Maker A quotes:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

Market Maker B enters with:

Bid:

## $99.50

Ask:

## $100.50

Market Maker B is offering better prices to both buyers and sellers.

Other market makers may respond.

Competition can compress the spread.

---

# Highly Competitive Markets Can Have Tiny Spreads

In very liquid markets, the difference may become extremely small.

This helps traders enter and exit more efficiently.

Small spreads are one reason high-liquidity markets are often cheaper to trade.

---

# Market Makers Also Take Risk

Market makers aren't simply collecting free spread.

They can lose money if price moves quickly.

Imagine a market maker buys at:

## $100

expecting to sell at:

## $100.10

But the market suddenly crashes to:

## $95.

Their inventory loses value.

This is one reason market makers may widen spreads during volatile periods.

---

# Why Spreads Widen During Volatility

Suppose major news hits.

Price starts moving rapidly.

Market makers face greater uncertainty.

They may:

- reduce order sizes
- move orders farther away
- cancel orders
- widen spreads

So a normally tight market can suddenly become expensive to trade.

---

# Example During Normal Conditions

BTC:

Bid:

## $100,000

Ask:

## $100,005

Spread:

## $5

---

# Example During Extreme Volatility

BTC:

Bid:

## $99,500

Ask:

## $100,500

Spread:

## $1,000

This is exaggerated for illustration, but it shows what liquidity stress can do.

---

# Low Liquidity + High Volatility Is a Dangerous Combination

Imagine a small crypto token has:

- few buyers
- few sellers
- large price swings

The spread can become enormous.

If you need to exit quickly:

## You may have to accept a very poor price.

---

# Spread Can Change in Seconds

Like the order book itself, spreads are dynamic.

One moment:

Bid:

## $9.99

Ask:

## $10.01

Next moment:

Bid:

## $9.50

Ask:

## $10.50

The market can change rapidly.

---

# Limit Orders Can Avoid Crossing the Spread Immediately

Suppose:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

Instead of market buying at $101, you place:

## Buy Limit at $99.50

Your order waits.

If someone sells to you:

## You may get a better price.

But there is a trade-off:

# Your order may never fill.

---

# Market Order vs Limit Order Revisited

### Market Order

Priority:

## Execution now.

You usually cross the spread.

### Limit Order

Priority:

## Your chosen price.

You may avoid paying the spread immediately.

But execution is uncertain.

---

# Makers vs Takers Revisited

A trader who places a limit order that waits can become a:

# **Maker**

They add liquidity.

A trader who immediately trades against an existing order is a:

# **Taker**

They remove liquidity.

Some exchanges charge different fees for:

- makers
- takers

because their effect on the order book differs.

---

# What Happens When the Bid and Ask Meet?

Imagine:

Best Bid:

## $10

Best Ask:

## $10

A buyer and seller agree on price.

The matching engine executes the trade.

That order disappears or reduces in size.

New best bid and ask prices are revealed from the remaining order book.

---

# Orders Can Be Partially Filled

Suppose a seller asks:

## $10

for:

## 1,000 tokens.

A buyer wants only:

## 300.

The trade executes for 300 tokens.

The seller still has:

## 700 tokens

remaining at $10.

This is a:

# **Partial Fill**

---

# Spread Can Be Different at Different Order Sizes

Top of book:

Bid:

## $99.99

Ask:

## $100.01

Looks excellent.

But suppose only:

## $10

of tokens are available there.

For a:

## $100,000 order

the effective spread across enough depth may be much wider.

Professional traders therefore care about:

# **Effective Spread**

and:

# **Executable Depth**

not only the first visible prices.

---

# What Is Effective Spread?

A simple way to think about effective spread is:

## What price difference does your actual trade experience?

A tiny trader may get near the top-of-book price.

A huge trader may need to consume many levels.

The real trading cost can therefore be much larger than the visible spread.

---

# Different Trading Pairs Can Have Different Spreads

Suppose a token trades against:

- USDC
- USDT
- BTC
- ETH

Each pair has its own:

- liquidity
- order book
- spread

TOKEN/USDC might have a tight market.

TOKEN/BTC might be extremely thin.

Same token.

Different market.

---

# Different Exchanges Can Have Different Spreads

Exchange A:

Bid:

## $99.99

Ask:

## $100.01

Exchange B:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

Exchange A currently offers a much tighter spread.

This can happen because of different:

- liquidity
- market makers
- user activity
- fees
- market conditions

---

# Arbitrage Helps Keep Prices Aligned

Suppose one exchange has:

Ask:

## $100

Another exchange has:

Bid:

## $105

An arbitrage trader may:

- buy around $100
- sell around $105

This activity helps pull the prices closer together.

Arbitrage is one mechanism that links otherwise separate markets.

---

# But Spread Can Destroy an Apparent Arbitrage

Imagine:

Exchange A displays:

## $100

Exchange B displays:

## $102

Looks like:

## $2 profit.

But then you check:

Exchange A Ask:

## $101.50

Exchange B Bid:

## $100.50

The executable prices actually offer:

## No profitable spread.

This is why professional trading uses:

# **Bid and Ask**

not simply headline prices.

---

# DEXs Don't Always Have a Traditional Bid and Ask

Classic automated market maker DEXs work differently.

Instead of a traditional order book containing bids and asks, traders swap against:

# **Liquidity Pools**

The pool calculates an execution price based on:

- reserves
- swap size
- AMM formula
- fees

---

# DEXs Still Have an Effective Spread

Even if a DEX doesn't display a conventional order book, there can still be a difference between:

- the effective price for buying
- the effective price for selling

This can result from:

- AMM curve
- fees
- price impact
- available liquidity

The mechanics differ, but immediate round-trip trading can still have a cost.

---

# Example of a DEX Round Trip

You swap:

## 1,000 USDC → TOKEN

Then immediately swap:

## TOKEN → USDC

Even if the external market does not move, you may receive less than:

## 1,000 USDC

because of:

- swap fees
- price impact
- pool mechanics

This is similar in spirit to paying a spread, although the underlying mechanism is different.

---

# Concentrated Liquidity Changes Effective Pricing

In concentrated-liquidity AMMs, large amounts of liquidity may exist around the current price.

This can produce very efficient pricing.

But if price moves outside those liquidity ranges:

## Depth can drop rapidly.

The effective cost of trading can then increase.

---

# Why Small DEX Pools Can Have Huge Effective Spreads

A tiny pool may not have enough liquidity to efficiently handle even moderate trades.

The quote for buying a token may look dramatically different from the quote for immediately selling it.

Always inspect:

- estimated output
- price impact
- pool liquidity

---

# The “Price” of a Token Is Really a Market

This is an important concept.

People often say:

> “Bitcoin is $100,000.”

But a functioning market actually contains:

## Buyers offering slightly less

and:

## Sellers asking slightly more.

The headline price compresses this entire market into one convenient number.

Underneath that number is:

# **An auction**

---

# Markets Are Continuous Auctions

Buyers compete with other buyers.

Sellers compete with other sellers.

When someone accepts the opposite side's price:

## A trade occurs.

The bid, ask and spread show the current state of that auction.

---

# Why Spread Matters for Scalpers

Suppose a trader is attempting to make:

## 0.2%

on a very short-term trade.

If the spread is:

## 1%

the trading strategy may already be in trouble before fees.

Small-target trading depends heavily on execution costs.

---

# Why Spread Matters Less for Some Long-Term Investors

If someone plans to hold an asset for:

## five years

a tiny 0.02% spread may be relatively unimportant compared with the long-term price movement.

But a:

## 10% spread

is significant for almost any trader.

Context matters.

---

# Percentage Spread Matters More Than Absolute Spread

Consider two markets.

### Asset A

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $100

Spread:

## $1

Approximately:

## 1%

### Asset B

Bid:

## $99,999

Ask:

## $100,000

Spread:

## $1

Approximately:

## 0.001%

Same dollar spread.

Completely different percentage cost.

---

# Calculate Percentage Spread

A simplified calculation is:

# **Spread ÷ Mid Price × 100**

Example:

Bid:

## $9

Ask:

## $11

Mid:

## $10

Spread:

## $2

Percentage spread:

## $2 ÷ $10 × 100 = 20%

That's an extremely wide market.

---

# Tight Spread Doesn't Guarantee Deep Liquidity

This is another trap.

Bid:

## $99.99

Ask:

## $100.01

Fantastic spread.

But only:

## one token

is available on either side.

The market is tight at the top but:

## Extremely shallow.

Always check both:

# Spread + Depth

---

# Deep Liquidity Doesn't Always Mean Tight Spread

It's also possible for significant liquidity to sit farther away from the current price.

For example:

Huge buy orders exist at:

## $90

Huge sell orders at:

## $110

but little in between.

There is plenty of capital waiting.

Yet the immediate spread remains wide.

---

# Spread and Market Quality

A healthy active market often has a combination of:

- tight spread
- strong depth
- high volume
- stable liquidity

No single metric tells you everything.

Together they give a much better picture.

---

# Beware Zero-Volume Markets With Old Prices

Suppose the last trade occurred:

## three days ago

at:

## $10.

The chart still displays $10.

But the order book now shows:

Bid:

## $3

Ask:

## $20

The headline price is almost meaningless for immediate execution.

This can happen in extremely inactive markets.

---

# Stale Prices

A:

# **Stale Price**

is a displayed price that no longer accurately represents current executable market conditions.

This can happen when:

- trading is extremely infrequent
- liquidity disappears
- the last trade is old

Always check current bids and asks in thin markets.

---

# Bid-Ask Bounce

In some markets, the last traded price may jump back and forth between:

- bid
- ask

even when the true market value has barely changed.

Example:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101

Trade at ask:

## $101

Next trade at bid:

## $99

Chart appears to fall:

## $2

But the underlying market spread never actually changed.

This is called:

# **Bid-Ask Bounce**

---

# This Can Distort Very Short-Term Charts

On illiquid markets, candles may look volatile simply because trades alternate between the bid and ask.

Not every tiny chart move represents a genuine shift in valuation.

Again:

## Liquidity context matters.

---

# Spread During a New Token Launch

New token markets can have:

- almost no sellers
- almost no buyers
- enormous spreads
- rapidly moving order books

Early displayed prices can therefore be extremely unstable.

A headline price should be treated carefully until meaningful liquidity develops.

---

# Spread During Market Crashes

During panic:

- buyers may withdraw orders
- sellers become aggressive
- market makers reduce risk
- spreads widen

This means the exact moment you most urgently want to exit may also be the moment execution becomes most expensive.

That is liquidity risk.

---

# Spread During Market Pumps

The same thing can happen upward.

A token suddenly pumps.

Sellers remove their asks.

Buyers aggressively chase price.

The spread may widen.

A market buy can execute much higher than expected.

---

# Stop Orders and Spread

Suppose your stop triggers when the displayed market reaches:

## $100.

But current bid is only:

## $98.

A stop-market sell may execute around:

## $98

or worse depending on depth.

The spread is one reason a stop trigger and final execution price can differ.

---

# Take-Profit Orders and Spread

Suppose your take-profit target is:

## $100.

Depending on the platform, the relevant trigger could be based on:

- last price
- mark price
- bid
- ask

The actual execution may still depend on available order-book liquidity.

Always know how the platform handles triggers.

---

# Limit Orders Can Sit Inside the Spread

Suppose:

Bid:

## $99

Ask:

## $101.

You could place a buy limit at:

## $100.

Your order now becomes the best bid.

New market:

Bid:

## $100

Ask:

## $101.

You have helped narrow the spread.

---

# Another Trader Can Improve Your Price

Someone else may place:

## $100.50 bid.

Now they become the best bidder.

This constant competition is how order books evolve.

---

# Market Makers Are Constantly Repricing

Automated systems may update quotes thousands of times as conditions change.

They monitor:

- other exchanges
- volatility
- inventory
- incoming orders
- market risk

The spread you see is the result of this constant competition and risk management.

# A Better Beginner Routine

Before placing a trade, check:

## 1. What is the best bid?

## 2. What is the best ask?

## 3. How wide is the spread?

## 4. What is the spread as a percentage?

## 5. How much liquidity exists at those prices?

## 6. How much depth exists beyond them?

## 7. How large is my order?

## 8. Could my own order cause slippage?

## 9. Is the displayed price the last price, midpoint or something else?

## 10. Is the market unusually volatile right now?

These checks can prevent a lot of unpleasant surprises.

## $80

## $100

## $20

# The Key Lesson

The bid, ask and spread show you the real immediate buying and selling market.

Remember:

### 🟢 BID

**The highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.**

### 🔴 ASK

**The lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.**

### ↔️ SPREAD

**The difference between the best bid and best ask.**

### 🎯 MID PRICE

**The halfway point between bid and ask — but not necessarily an executable price.**

### 📊 LAST PRICE

**The price of the most recent completed trade.**

Most importantly:

# **The price shown on the screen is not always the price you can actually trade at.**

If you want to buy immediately:

## Look at the ask.

If you want to sell immediately:

## Look at the bid.

And if the gap between them is huge:

## Pay attention.

That spread is telling you something about the market you're about to trade.
