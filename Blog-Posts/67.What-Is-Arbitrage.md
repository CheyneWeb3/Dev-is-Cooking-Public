---

# What Is Arbitrage?

## **Arbitrage**

is the process of attempting to profit from a price difference for the same or economically related asset across different markets.

The basic idea is:

# **Buy where it is cheaper**

and:

# **Sell where it is more expensive**

If both trades can be completed successfully and the price difference is larger than all costs:

## the trader may capture the difference as profit.

---

# Simple Arbitrage Example

Imagine TOKEN X trades at:

### Exchange A

## $10

### Exchange B

## $11

A trader might:

## Buy 100 TOKEN on Exchange A

Cost:

## $1,000

Then:

## Sell 100 TOKEN on Exchange B

Revenue:

## $1,100

Before costs:

## $100 difference

That looks like:

# **$100 gross arbitrage profit**

---

# But That Is Not Yet Real Profit

You still need to subtract:

- trading fees
- withdrawal fees
- network fees
- slippage
- price impact
- borrowing costs if applicable
- transaction costs

If those costs total:

## $40

then the approximate net result becomes:

## $60

Not $100.

---

# Gross Arbitrage vs Net Arbitrage

This distinction matters.

### Gross Arbitrage

Price difference before costs.

### Net Arbitrage

What remains after all costs.

A large-looking arbitrage can disappear once execution costs are included.

---

# Arbitrage Is About Executable Prices

Suppose:

Exchange A headline price:

## $10

Exchange B headline price:

## $11

Looks like:

## 10% difference

But then you check the real order books.

Exchange A:

Best Ask:

## $10.80

Exchange B:

Best Bid:

## $10.90

Now the actual executable difference is only:

## $0.10

The headline prices exaggerated the opportunity.

---

# Use Ask When Buying

If you're buying immediately:

## You care about the ask.

Suppose the chart says:

## $10

but the cheapest seller is:

## $10.25

Your actual purchase starts around:

## $10.25

not $10.

---

# Use Bid When Selling

If you're selling immediately:

## You care about the bid.

Suppose the chart says:

## $11

but the highest buyer is:

## $10.70

Your sale starts around:

## $10.70

not $11.

---

# The Real Arbitrage Spread

So instead of comparing:

## Last Price A vs Last Price B

a trader should generally care about:

# **Buy Ask vs Sell Bid**

For example:

Buy market ask:

## $10.25

Sell market bid:

## $10.70

Executable spread:

## $0.45

That is a much more useful starting point.

---

# Why Can the Same Asset Have Different Prices?

Because markets are separate.

Exchange A has its own:

- buyers
- sellers
- liquidity
- order book

Exchange B has another.

DEX Pool A has its own:

- reserves
- liquidity providers
- recent swaps

DEX Pool B has different reserves.

There is no magical universal database forcing every market to show exactly the same price at every millisecond.

---

# Arbitrage Helps Connect Separate Markets

If TOKEN X becomes cheap on Market A and expensive on Market B, arbitrage traders may:

## Buy on A

and:

## Sell on B.

Their buying pressure on A pushes the cheap market upward.

Their selling pressure on B pushes the expensive market downward.

The two prices begin moving toward each other.

---

# Example

Market A:

## $90

Market B:

## $100

Arbitrage traders buy Market A.

Demand pushes it:

## $90 → $94 → $97

At the same time, they sell Market B.

Selling pushes it:

## $100 → $98 → $97

The markets converge around:

## $97

The price difference disappears.

---

# Arbitrage Can Help Keep Markets Efficient

This is one of arbitrage's important economic roles.

Arbitrage traders are motivated by profit.

But their activity can also help reduce:

# **Price discrepancies**

between markets.

This is why large liquid markets usually trade at very similar prices across major venues.

---

# Perfect Price Alignment Is Rare

Even highly efficient markets can show tiny differences because:

- prices constantly move
- exchanges process trades independently
- network latency exists
- liquidity differs
- fees differ

Arbitrage activity often keeps those differences relatively small.

---

# Arbitrage Opportunities Can Exist for Milliseconds

In active markets, automated trading systems watch prices continuously.

When an opportunity appears:

## bots may react almost instantly.

By the time a human manually notices and clicks:

## the opportunity may already be gone.

---

# Manual Arbitrage vs Automated Arbitrage

### Manual Arbitrage

A person watches markets and manually places trades.

### Automated Arbitrage

Software continuously:

- reads prices
- calculates opportunities
- checks costs
- executes trades

Automation is common because many arbitrage opportunities are:

## small and short-lived.

---

# Arbitrage Does Not Require a Price Prediction

This makes arbitrage conceptually different from ordinary directional trading.

A directional trader might say:

> “I think Bitcoin will rise.”

An arbitrage trader might say:

> “Bitcoin is currently cheaper on Market A than Market B.”

The focus is not necessarily:

## Where price goes tomorrow.

The focus is:

## The current relative mispricing.

---

# Arbitrage Can Still Have Risk

People sometimes call arbitrage:

## “Risk-free profit.”

That description is usually too simplistic in real trading.

Practical risks include:

- one side executing while the other fails
- prices changing
- withdrawals being delayed
- liquidity disappearing
- transaction failure
- network congestion
- fees increasing
- smart contract risk

So real arbitrage often involves:

# **Execution Risk**

---

# What Is Execution Risk?

Suppose you buy TOKEN at:

## $10

expecting to immediately sell elsewhere for:

## $11.

But before your second trade executes:

## the sell price falls to $9.80.

Your expected arbitrage disappears.

You are now holding a directional position whether you wanted one or not.

---

# Leg Risk

When an arbitrage requires multiple separate transactions, each transaction can be called a:

## **Leg**

If one leg completes and another fails, the trader is exposed.

This is often called:

# **Leg Risk**

---

# Example

Leg 1:

Buy TOKEN at:

## $10

Success.

Leg 2:

Sell TOKEN at:

## $10.50

Fails.

Now you simply own TOKEN.

If TOKEN falls:

## you can lose money.

The trade stopped being neutral.

---

# Atomic Arbitrage

On some blockchain systems, smart contracts can execute multiple actions within:

# **One transaction**

If the entire sequence cannot complete:

## the transaction reverts.

This is commonly described as:

# **Atomic Execution**

---

# Why Atomic Execution Is Powerful

Suppose a smart contract attempts:

1. Buy TOKEN on DEX A
2. Sell TOKEN on DEX B
3. Verify the result is profitable

If any required step fails:

## the entire transaction may revert

depending on how the contract is written.

This can reduce some forms of leg risk.

---

# But Atomic Arbitrage Still Has Costs

Even if a reverted transaction protects the trading capital from an incomplete sequence, the trader may still incur:

- gas costs
- failed transaction costs
- opportunity costs

Atomic does not mean:

## Completely free of risk or cost.

---

# DEX Arbitrage

Decentralised exchanges create many arbitrage opportunities because liquidity is spread across:

- different DEXs
- different pools
- different fee tiers
- different AMM designs

The same token can therefore trade at slightly different pool prices.

---

# Simple DEX Arbitrage Example

DEX A:

## TOKEN = 1.00 USDC

DEX B:

## TOKEN = 1.10 USDC

A trader may attempt:

## Buy TOKEN on DEX A

then:

## Sell TOKEN on DEX B

If execution costs are lower than the price difference:

## a profit may exist.

---

# Arbitrage Changes the Pools

Suppose you buy TOKEN from DEX A.

That purchase:

- removes TOKEN from the pool
- adds the quote asset
- pushes TOKEN's pool price upward

Then you sell TOKEN into DEX B.

That sale:

- adds TOKEN
- removes the quote asset
- pushes TOKEN's price downward

The two pool prices move closer together.

---

# Arbitrage Is Part of AMM Price Discovery

An AMM pool does not independently know:

> “The correct global price.”

It only knows its:

- reserves
- formula
- swaps

If a pool price differs from other markets:

# **Arbitrage traders can correct it through trading.**

This is a major part of how separate DEX pools stay aligned.

---

# Example: Pool Starts at the Wrong Price

Imagine TOKEN is broadly trading around:

## $1

A new pool is created with reserves implying:

## $2 per TOKEN.

The pool is mispriced relative to the broader market.

Arbitrage traders may:

## Sell TOKEN into the expensive pool.

As they do:

- TOKEN reserves increase
- quote reserves decrease
- TOKEN's pool price falls

Eventually the pool may approach the wider market price.

---

# The Arbitrage Traders Did Not “Set” the Correct Price

They responded to the price difference.

Their trading changed the pool reserves.

The resulting price became closer to prices available elsewhere.

That distinction matters.

---

# DEX Arbitrage Can Happen Between Versions of the Same DEX

You might have:

- V2 pool
- V3 pool
- another fee tier
- another pool implementation

The same pair may exist in several places.

If their prices diverge enough:

## arbitrage may connect them.

---

# Triangular Arbitrage

Arbitrage does not always involve two markets.

It can also involve multiple trading pairs.

This is called:

# **Triangular Arbitrage**

---

# Simple Triangular Example

Imagine markets for:

## BTC/USDC

## ETH/BTC

## ETH/USDC

If those three prices become inconsistent, a trader may cycle:

## USDC → BTC → ETH → USDC

If the final amount is greater than the starting amount after costs:

## an arbitrage opportunity existed.

---

# Example

Start:

## 10,000 USDC

Trade:

## USDC → BTC

then:

## BTC → ETH

then:

## ETH → USDC

Finish:

## 10,100 USDC

Gross difference:

## 100 USDC

Then subtract fees and execution costs.

---

# Triangular Arbitrage Can Exist on One Exchange

Because the three pairs may exist on the same venue, there may be no need to transfer assets between exchanges.

Automated systems can monitor the relationships continuously.

Again, opportunities are often very small and temporary.

---

# Cross-Exchange Arbitrage

This involves separate venues.

Example:

## BTC cheaper on Exchange A

and:

## BTC more expensive on Exchange B.

The simplest version would be:

- buy A
- transfer BTC
- sell B

But there is a problem.

---

# Transfers Take Time

By the time the asset arrives on Exchange B:

## the price difference may be gone.

This creates:

# **Transfer Risk**

Fast-moving crypto markets can change significantly while a blockchain transfer is pending.

---

# Pre-Funded Arbitrage

Professional traders may avoid waiting for each transfer.

Instead they keep capital on:

## both exchanges.

For example:

Exchange A:

## USDC

Exchange B:

## TOKEN

When the opportunity appears:

- buy TOKEN on A
- sell TOKEN on B

Both trades happen almost simultaneously.

Balances can be rebalanced later.

---

# This Reduces Transfer Delay

The trader doesn't need to move the purchased asset before completing the sell.

But it introduces other considerations:

- capital locked across venues
- exchange counterparty risk
- rebalancing costs

There is always a trade-off.

---

# CEX-to-DEX Arbitrage

Price differences can also occur between:

## Centralised Exchanges

and:

## Decentralised Exchanges.

Example:

CEX:

## TOKEN = $10

DEX:

## TOKEN = $11

Arbitrage traders may attempt to buy on the CEX and sell on the DEX, or use existing inventory on both sides.

---

# Different Settlement Systems Add Complexity

CEX trades happen inside an exchange's internal ledger.

DEX trades happen on-chain.

That means CEX-DEX arbitrage may involve:

- deposits
- withdrawals
- network confirmation times
- smart contract execution
- gas
- bridging

This makes execution more complicated.

---

# Arbitrage Between Stablecoins

Even assets designed to trade around the same value can temporarily diverge.

For example:

Stablecoin A:

## $1.00

Stablecoin B:

## $0.98

If traders believe both should converge and markets permit efficient conversion, arbitrage may help narrow the gap.

But stablecoins carry their own:

## depeg and credit risks.

---

# A “Stablecoin Arbitrage” Is Not Automatically Safe

If a stablecoin trades at:

## $0.80

there may be a reason.

Perhaps:

- reserves are questioned
- redemption is unavailable
- liquidity has disappeared
- issuer risk increased

Buying at $0.80 because:

> “It should be worth $1”

is not automatically risk-free arbitrage.

You must understand the mechanism connecting it to $1.

---

# True Arbitrage vs Convergence Trading

This distinction is useful.

### Arbitrage

Usually aims to capture an existing price mismatch with offsetting trades.

### Convergence Trade

May depend on two prices eventually moving closer together.

The second involves more market risk because convergence may not happen quickly — or at all.

---

# Arbitrage and Liquidity

Suppose TOKEN is:

## $10 on A

## $15 on B

Looks like:

## 50% difference.

But Market A only has:

## 2 TOKEN available at $10.

After that:

## next ask = $14.90.

The actual arbitrage size is tiny.

---

# Headline Spread Is Not Enough

You need to know:

# **How much can actually be bought cheaply?**

and:

# **How much can actually be sold expensively?**

That is where market depth becomes critical.

---

# Depth-Adjusted Arbitrage

Suppose:

DEX A:

First 100 TOKEN average:

## $10

Next 1,000 TOKEN average:

## $11

DEX B:

First 100 TOKEN sell price:

## $12

Next 1,000 TOKEN average:

## $10.80

There may be profit for:

## 100 TOKEN

but not:

## 1,000 TOKEN.

Arbitrage size matters.

---

# Bigger Is Not Always Better

Increasing arbitrage size can create more gross revenue.

But it also creates:

- more price impact
- more slippage
- less favourable average prices

At some point, increasing size can reduce or eliminate profit.

---

# Optimal Trade Size

Professional arbitrage systems may calculate:

# **The trade size that maximises expected net profit**

rather than simply using the largest possible amount.

This depends on:

- liquidity curves
- order-book depth
- fees
- gas
- available capital

---

# Arbitrage Can Consume Its Own Opportunity

This is especially obvious in AMMs.

Suppose a pool is underpriced.

You buy from it.

Your purchase pushes the price upward.

Each additional unit becomes less profitable.

Eventually the price difference is too small to cover costs.

The arbitrage has:

# **Corrected itself through execution**

---

# Fees Matter

Suppose:

Buy:

## $100

Sell:

## $101

Gross spread:

## 1%

But:

Buy fee:

## 0.4%

Sell fee:

## 0.4%

Other costs:

## 0.3%

Total:

## 1.1%

You lose money despite the apparent 1% arbitrage.

---

# Gas Matters on DEXs

On-chain arbitrage may require:

- one swap
- multiple swaps
- flash loan
- repayment
- contract execution

All of that consumes gas.

A:

## $5 theoretical profit

is useless if execution costs:

## $10.

---

# Fees Can Change With Network Conditions

During blockchain congestion:

## gas prices can increase rapidly.

An arbitrage that was profitable a minute ago may become unprofitable because transaction costs changed.

---

# Slippage Matters

You calculate:

Buy at:

## $10

Sell at:

## $10.50

But your actual average prices become:

Buy:

## $10.20

Sell:

## $10.30

Gross edge:

## only $0.10

Then fees may remove the rest.

---

# Price Impact Matters

On DEXs, your own swap changes pool reserves.

If the trade is too large:

## your own execution can erase the arbitrage.

The quote must be calculated for:

# **Your actual trade size**

---

# Latency Matters

## **Latency**

is delay.

This can include:

- network delay
- RPC response time
- exchange API delay
- blockchain block time
- transaction propagation

Arbitrage opportunities can disappear during these delays.

---

# Stale Quotes Are Dangerous

Suppose your system reads:

DEX A:

## $10

DEX B:

## $11

But the DEX A quote is:

## 2 seconds old.

In a volatile market, $10 may no longer exist.

Your calculated opportunity is based on stale information.

---

# Fast Markets Reward Fast Repricing

Professional systems continuously refresh:

- reserves
- order books
- quotes
- gas costs

They calculate whether the opportunity still exists:

## right before execution.

Because in arbitrage:

# **Old prices are useless prices.**

---

# Competition Matters

You are rarely the only person watching a price difference.

Other arbitrage traders may see the same opportunity.

If they execute first:

## they change the market.

Your opportunity may disappear.

---

# The First Trade Can Remove the Profit

Suppose DEX A is underpriced.

Bot A buys first.

That pushes the price upward.

Bot B's planned trade arrives moments later.

Now there may be:

## no profitable spread left.

This is part of arbitrage competition.

---

# Arbitrage and MEV

On public blockchains, profitable transaction opportunities can attract:

# **MEV — Maximal Extractable Value**

searchers.

These actors may compete to:

- arbitrage pools
- reorder eligible transactions
- capture liquidation opportunities

We'll cover MEV properly in Lesson #70.

---

# Arbitrage Can Occur After Large Swaps

Suppose a user makes a huge buy on DEX A.

That pushes TOKEN from:

## $10 → $12

But other markets remain around:

## $10.

Now DEX A is expensive.

Arbitrage traders may sell TOKEN into DEX A.

That pushes its price back down.

---

# This Is How AMM Prices Recover After Large Trades

A large trader temporarily moves the pool away from the broader market.

Arbitrage traders then trade against the difference.

The result is:

## price rebalancing.

---

# Arbitrage Is Not Creating Value From Nothing

The arbitrage profit usually comes from:

# **The price discrepancy**

Someone was willing to trade one market at a price inconsistent with another available market.

The arbitrage trader takes on execution work and risk to capture the difference.

---

# Who Pays the Arbitrage Profit?

There isn't always one simple person who can be labelled:

## “the loser.”

Depending on the situation, the value may arise from:

- a trader who moved an AMM price
- stale liquidity
- separate market participants
- temporary supply/demand imbalance

The mechanism varies.

---

# Arbitrage Can Benefit Liquidity Providers Too

In AMMs, arbitrage trades generate:

## swap fees.

Liquidity providers may earn those fees.

However, arbitrage can also interact with:

## impermanent loss

and changing asset values.

So the economics are more complicated than:

> “Arbitrage is bad for LPs.”

---

# Arbitrage Can Be Very Frequent

In active crypto markets, arbitrage is not necessarily some rare unusual event.

Automated systems can perform it continuously whenever small pricing differences become large enough to cover costs.

---

# Tiny Differences Can Matter at Large Scale

Suppose arbitrage profit is only:

## 0.05%

On a:

## $1 million

trade, gross difference is:

## $500

before costs.

Professional strategies may target many small opportunities instead of waiting for huge visible mispricings.

---

# But Scale Is Limited by Liquidity

You cannot simply say:

> “If $1,000 makes $10 profit, I'll trade $1 million and make $10,000.”

The larger trade may:

- move price
- consume depth
- increase slippage

The opportunity may not scale linearly.

---

# Flash Loans and Arbitrage

In DeFi, some protocols allow:

# **Flash Loans**

These allow assets to be borrowed and repaid within the same atomic transaction, subject to the protocol's rules and fees.

This can allow an arbitrage trader to access temporary liquidity without holding the entire principal beforehand.

---

# Simple Flash-Loan Arbitrage Concept

Within one transaction:

1. Borrow asset
2. Use it in an arbitrage
3. Receive the proceeds
4. Repay borrowed amount plus required fee
5. Keep any remaining profit

If the transaction cannot satisfy repayment:

## it generally reverts under the flash-loan mechanism.

---

# Flash Loans Do Not Create the Profit

This distinction matters.

The flash loan provides:

## temporary capital.

The profit still has to come from:

## a genuine arbitrage opportunity.

If the price difference doesn't cover:

- swap fees
- gas
- flash-loan fee
- price impact

there is no profitable trade.

---

# More Capital Does Not Fix a Bad Arbitrage

If an opportunity loses:

## 1%

after costs,

borrowing more money simply scales up the loss.

Capital is not the edge.

The price discrepancy and execution quality are the edge.

---

# Flash Loans Can Reduce Capital Requirements

Without a flash loan, a trader may need:

## $1 million

sitting idle to execute a $1 million arbitrage.

With appropriate DeFi infrastructure, temporary transaction-scoped borrowing may reduce that requirement.

That is one reason flash loans became closely associated with DeFi arbitrage.

---

# Arbitrage Can Happen Without Flash Loans

This is equally important.

Most arbitrage concepts do not require:

## flash loans.

A trader can use:

- owned capital
- pre-funded exchange balances
- borrowed capital
- inventory

Flash loans are just one possible funding mechanism.

---

# Arbitrage vs Market Making

These are related but different.

### Arbitrage

Attempts to capture differences between markets or related prices.

### Market Making

Continuously provides buy and sell liquidity and attempts to earn from spreads and other incentives.

A firm can do both.

But they are not the same strategy.

---

# Arbitrage vs Speculation

### Speculation

> “I believe this asset will increase.”

### Arbitrage

> “The asset is currently cheaper here than there.”

Speculation relies more directly on future directional movement.

Arbitrage focuses on current relative pricing.

---

# Arbitrage vs Hedging

### Hedging

Reduces or offsets an existing risk.

### Arbitrage

Attempts to capture a pricing discrepancy.

A trade can sometimes have characteristics of both, but the goals are different.

---

# Why Arbitrage Opportunities Exist at All

If arbitrage corrects prices, why doesn't it eliminate every difference instantly?

Because arbitrage has costs and constraints.

These include:

- fees
- liquidity
- latency
- capital
- network delays
- withdrawal restrictions
- smart contract risk

A difference may need to become large enough before it is profitable to correct.

---

# Arbitrage Threshold

Suppose total execution cost is:

## 0.5%

A price difference of:

## 0.2%

isn't worth taking.

A difference of:

## 1%

might be.

The minimum profitable discrepancy acts like an:

# **Arbitrage Threshold**

---

# Different Traders Have Different Thresholds

Trader A may have:

- lower fees
- better infrastructure
- faster execution

Trader B may have:

- higher fees
- slower execution

A 0.3% difference might be profitable for A but not B.

This is why professional infrastructure matters.

---

# Withdrawal Restrictions Can Create Persistent Differences

Suppose a token trades:

## 10% higher

on Exchange B.

Normally arbitrage should close the gap.

But if:

## deposits are disabled,

traders cannot easily move tokens into Exchange B to sell them.

The price difference can persist much longer.

---

# Capital Controls Can Create Price Differences Too

Historically, crypto prices have sometimes differed across regions because of:

- banking restrictions
- capital controls
- local demand
- limited cross-border movement

A price difference is only arbitrageable if traders can actually execute the required flow.

---

# Not Every Price Difference Is Free Money

This is one of the biggest lessons.

If you see:

## TOKEN = $10 here

and:

## TOKEN = $20 there

do not immediately conclude:

> “Free 100% profit.”

Ask:

- Can I buy?
- Can I sell?
- Are deposits open?
- Are withdrawals open?
- Is it the same token contract?
- Is liquidity real?
- What are the fees?
- How much size is executable?

There may be a reason the discrepancy exists.

---

# Make Sure It Is the Same Asset

Crypto contains many assets with similar:

- names
- symbols

For example, multiple unrelated tokens can use the same ticker.

Before comparing prices:

# **Verify the actual contract or asset identifier.**

Buying the wrong token because the ticker matches is not arbitrage.

---

# Bridged Tokens Need Extra Attention

The same symbol may exist on multiple chains through:

- native issuance
- bridges
- wrapped representations

Their redemption mechanisms and risks can differ.

You must understand exactly what asset is being compared.

---

# Arbitrage on DEXs Needs Token-Safety Awareness

A token may appear to have a huge price difference because:

- it has transfer taxes
- selling is restricted
- the contract blocks certain addresses
- unusual token mechanics exist

A quote alone does not guarantee successful execution.

---

# Transfer Taxes Can Destroy Arbitrage

Suppose apparent spread:

## 10%

But token transfer tax:

## 8%

Then add:

- swap fees
- gas
- slippage

The opportunity may disappear entirely.

---

# Honeypot and Restriction Risk

Some malicious tokens may allow buying but make selling difficult or impossible.

A displayed price difference is meaningless if the token cannot actually be sold through the intended route.

Always understand token mechanics before assuming an arbitrage exists.

---

# DEX Routes Can Include Multiple Hops

An arbitrage route might be:

## USDC → TOKEN → ETH → USDC

rather than simply:

## TOKEN A → TOKEN B.

Each additional hop adds:

- fees
- execution complexity
- price impact

The full route must still finish profitably.

---

# Route Profit Is What Matters

A route might contain one highly profitable-looking trade and one bad trade.

The only useful question is:

# **What is the final amount after the complete cycle?**

Start:

## 10,000 USDC

End:

## 10,050 USDC

Then subtract gas and other costs.

---

# Closed-Loop Arbitrage

A useful arbitrage structure begins and ends with the same asset.

For example:

## USDC → TOKEN A → TOKEN B → USDC

Starting and ending in USDC makes the result easier to measure.

Profit:

## Final USDC - Starting USDC - costs

---

# Arbitrage Profit Formula

Conceptually:

# **Net Profit = Final Value − Starting Value − All Costs**

Costs can include:

- exchange fees
- DEX swap fees
- gas
- borrowing fees
- transfer fees
- slippage

This is the number that matters.

---

# Example

Start:

## $10,000

Final value:

## $10,150

Gross gain:

## $150

Gas:

## $25

Trading fees:

## $40

Other costs:

## $10

Net:

## $75

The arbitrage made:

# **$75 net**

not $150.

---

# Failed Transactions Matter

Suppose you submit ten arbitrage transactions.

Nine fail.

Each costs:

## $5

The tenth succeeds and makes:

## $60

Gross successful profit:

## $60

Failed transaction costs:

## $45

Actual result:

## $15

Operational costs matter.

---

# Profit Per Trade Is Not Enough

An arbitrage system should consider:

- successful profits
- failed transaction costs
- infrastructure costs
- capital utilisation

A strategy that looks profitable trade-by-trade may be poor overall if failed attempts are expensive.

---

# Arbitrage Can Become Unprofitable Before Confirmation

On-chain systems may have a delay between:

## detecting an opportunity

and:

## transaction inclusion.

During that time:

- another trader acts
- reserves change
- gas changes
- the market moves

By execution time, the original calculation may be obsolete.

---

# Slippage Protection Matters

A transaction can specify limits on acceptable execution.

If the market moves too far:

## the trade can revert instead of executing at a terrible price.

That protects against bad execution.

But failed transactions may still cost gas.

---

# Arbitrage Bots Are Not Guaranteed Money Machines

A bot can be:

- too slow
- badly priced
- using stale data
- overpaying gas
- ignoring fees
- choosing bad trade sizes

Automation only executes the logic you give it.

It does not make unprofitable logic profitable.

# Good Arbitrage Is Mostly About Accuracy and Execution

The concept:

> “Buy cheaper, sell higher”

is easy.

The difficult part is correctly determining:

- real executable prices
- real liquidity
- optimal size
- all costs
- whether execution can finish safely

That is where most of the work lives.

# A Better Beginner Arbitrage Checklist

Before calling something an arbitrage, ask:

## 1. Is it definitely the same asset?

## 2. What is the real buy price?

## 3. What is the real sell price?

## 4. How much size exists at those prices?

## 5. What are the trading fees?

## 6. What is the expected slippage?

## 7. What is the price impact?

## 8. What are the network or gas costs?

## 9. Can both sides execute quickly enough?

## 10. What is the final net profit after everything?

Only then do you know whether the arbitrage is real.

# The Key Lesson

Arbitrage is the attempt to profit from a price difference between markets.

Remember:

### 🛒 BUY SIDE

**Buy where the asset is cheaper.**

### 💰 SELL SIDE

**Sell where the asset is more expensive.**

### ↔️ PRICE DISCREPANCY

**The difference between markets creates the potential opportunity.**

### 💧 LIQUIDITY

**Determines how much of that opportunity can actually be traded.**

### 💸 COSTS

**Fees, gas, slippage and price impact reduce profit.**

### ⚡ EXECUTION

**The opportunity only matters if both sides can actually complete.**

Most importantly:

# **Arbitrage is not about finding different numbers on two screens.**

It is about finding:

# **A real, executable price difference that remains profitable after every cost.**

That difference might last seconds.

Or milliseconds.

And every arbitrage trade helps push separate markets closer together.
